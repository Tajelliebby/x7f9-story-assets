The cold was constant. It always was.

Down here, in the fathomless belly of the deep, the dark pressed in from every direction, a steady, full-bodied embrace that knew every ridge of his scales and every hollow between his bones. He had grown up in that pressure. Learned to breathe inside it. It was the only thing in his long life that had never lied to him.

Einarr moved upward. Why? He did not know. The question had repeated itself through the depths, unanswered.

His tail swept in long, unhurried arcs. The water tasted of iron and old stone here, mineral-heavy, cold enough to ache where he was not built for aching. Schools of deep-dwellers scattered at his approach, their heat signatures blooming outward in pale orange bursts. A startled corona of small, stupid things. Others held. Circled lower. Watched the shape of him with that particular patient hunger that all predators share.

He didn’t slow for them.

The pressure hugged him tighter as he climbed, the way a familiar hand might grip a shoulder in greeting, firm and presumptuous. His hair fanned long and slow around him, drifting like sea-silk, catching the faint thermal eddies that rose from somewhere far below. He could feel the shape of the water changing against his gill-slits, could feel the temperature gradient before he saw the shimmer of it, that broad, trembling curtain strung between the abyss and the world above.

Melusine’s Veil.

He paused beneath it. Old habit.

The ring on his right hand pulsed once, steady and full, charged to the rim by the fathoms of pressure stacked above him. He pressed two fingers against the Veil. It shivered. The dense cold of the deep recoiled from the lighter, warmer water on the other side, and for a moment the membrane between them drummed faintly against his palm like a held breath.

He passed through.

The change was immediate. Not warmth, not truly, but the particular sensation of cold becoming less than cold. The mineral bite on his tongue thinning to something softer and less certain. The currents here were nervous, shivering things compared to the slow, deliberate roll of the deep. They moved in shorter strokes, changed direction without warning, nudged at the ends of his hair and snagged the edge of his tail with small, argumentative pressure. He tracked the scatter of heat signatures above him, abundant and restless, the water crowded in a way the dark was not.

He kept climbing.

His ring pulsed again, slower now, recalibrating as the pressure unwound from his body in gradual degrees. He flexed his hand against the drag of ascending water, watched the warmth bloom at his fingertips in soft copper tones, watched the shadows above him begin to differentiate, to pull apart into layers of motion. Something vast and rhythmic moved up there, a long, rocking thing with a hull of dense cold wood and iron fittings that radiated a faint, industrial warmth.

A vessel.

He slowed.

The next change arrived before he was prepared for it, as it always did no matter how many decades separated his ascents. The water simply let go. The water loosened around him like a thing unraveling. The cold had thinned to something almost bearable. The taste of salt sharpened.

He broke the surface.

The storm hit him everywhere at once.

Wind drove into his face and chest like a slap from something enormous and thoughtless. The cold up here was a different animal than the cold below; it had teeth, it moved, it found the wet places at the back of his neck and the soft webbing between his fingers and bit down.

Crash!

The heavens above lit up. The temperature spike was brief, brilliant, burning. It faded almost immediately, replaced by the more measured warmth of electrical discharge still radiating through the surrounding sea. Lightning. That was the word the humans used.

He blinked. Let his eyes adjust.

The vessel was close. Much closer than its shadow suggested. A large ship, wide-bellied and listing, its hull slamming against waves with a resonance Einarr felt in his sternum each time. The water between him and it was violent, rolling swells that rose and fell in quick succession, not the long deep-ocean swells he knew, but the short, vicious chop of a narrow and angry strait. He could feel the pull of competing currents beneath him.

Something cracked. Then groaned. The ship was losing.

There was a particular entertainment in the noises of a ship in distress, the chorus of stressed timber and screaming iron, the way the hull protested before it gave. He had heard it before. Likely he would hear it again. The sea dogs on deck swarmed their vessel’s ropes and rails, orange heat-shapes blurring in the rain, their voices rising and threading together into that chaotic, guttural dialect he had never had patience for.

He was already drifting back.

Then the lightning came, and this time he felt the sharp thermal bloom followed immediately by a massive cold shock where the heated sky collapsed into the sea. The impact reverberated through the water, through his bones.

“Fire.” A voice, thin and desperate, cutting through the wind.

He sensed them scrambling across the deck. Small, frantic heat signatures moving like prey-fish sensing the arrival of a predator. They moved with the kind of desperation that meant they understood, on some level, what was coming. Another crack, more splintering, the thermal wake of the ship as it lifted, suspended for a moment in the chaos, and then crashed against the rocks that jutted from the Strait’s throat like teeth. The impact sent a shockwave through the water that made his gills flare.

Then a scream. Different from the others. Higher.

A body plummeted through the spray, before the wooden contraction lost its fight. Einarr watched as it was immediately swallowed by the current. The human bobbed to the surface, gasping. Not dead yet, then.

He was moving before he decided to. The water closed over him, the currents let him pass with little resistance. He found her in under a minute. She had not drowned, pointlessly clinging to something while the sea heaved. Every time a wave rolled over her she went under and then she came back up again, coughing, gasping, and held on.

He circled once. Watched.

The other sea dogs were farther out, voices already swallowed by the strait’s indifference. He noted their direction; the current was dispersing them. They would not have long.

He turned back to the small one. She was barely conscious, her heartbeat a frantic staccato he could feel through the water itself. Despite himself, he reached out, caught what he hoped was a wrist and pulled. She fought him for one instinctive, furious second before she seemed to register that the current was moving the wrong direction and his grip was not. She coughed, convulsed, clung to whatever part of him she could find. Her body was strange against his hands. Her skin was warm despite the cold, impossibly warm. Soft in a way that made him pause. Not like his people. She was squishy. Yielding. He had touched human skin before, in the way one touches most novel things, briefly and with mild interest. This was the same. He was almost certain it was the same.

He pulled her into a relatively still pocket of water, where the reef rose and offered protection from the worst of the surge. She was shaking, violent, uncontrollable shivers that wracked her entire frame. He held on another moment before he released her.

He dived before he thought better of it.

The water folded over him and he dropped fast, following the pull of depth until the noise of the surface thinned to almost nothing. Then he sang. The first note came from low in his chest, a long descending whistle that vibrated in his gill-slits and travelled outward through the water in slow rings.

Hval-Bein mót, Djúp-Vaggr slepp.

Three sharp clicks followed, precise as a lock being turned. He felt the song meet his own bones. The pain arrived promptly. It began at the base of his tail, a deep, rhythmic tremor that moved upward through each vertebra in sequence, not violent, not yet, just deliberate. The serpentine length of him began to shift. He could feel the mineral weight of his bones going soft, the dense cartilage of the deep-sea skeleton liquefying inward, beginning the long architecture of rearrangement. The scales along his lower half went tight then tighter, flushed through with an uncomfortable heat. The ring pulsed steadily on his hand. He focused on that. The second phase came before the first had finished. He sang it through clenched teeth.

Skel-húð dalar, líf-blóð flæðir.

A short sharp whistle, then the slow bone-clang that resonated more in his chest than his throat. The heat along his collarbone became something he would not call searing except that was the only word that fit. The micro-scales along his torso receded in a wave, a thousand small retreating edges, and the skin beneath thickened, pulling taut and then settling into something denser, built for a world that breathed air instead of water. He felt the ring’s charge drain a measure.

He kept climbing through it. The final movement was the worst. Always was. He opened his mouth and the third song came out half-involuntary, high and wild, a rising whistle that crested into a frequency he felt more in his jaw than his ears.

Gála-Hljóð opnar, loftið kallar.

His gills snapped shut, all at once, a sensation like a door slamming in a room that had always been open. There was a terrible, vertiginous moment where nothing breathed at all. Then his lungs opened. He broke the surface gasping, different from before, louder, more animal. The air hit like a flood. Too thin, too dry, too full of the particular delicacy of surface oxygen, but his body remembered the shape of it even when he didn’t.

The wave took him to shore and he stumbled. Sand beneath the soles of his feet crackling a prickly sensation. He stood for approximately four seconds before his knees had a different opinion. He caught himself on one hand, the ring cold against wet rock, and stayed there breathing. The belt around his hips chimed as the small bottles knocked together in the swell of wind. He found the right one by shape, uncorked it, drank. Warmth moved through him immediately, from the throat outward, smoothing the residual tremor in his hands and the spinning sensation behind his eyes.

He found her.

She had not moved. Half-draped across her piece of timber. Her breathing came in sharp gasps, and her thermal signature was dimming with each passing moment. Not good for a human at all. He got his hands under her, heavy with soaked wool, and carried her to a shallow cut in the rock face above the waterline. The wind still found them there, but the rain didn’t. He set her down.

He settled beside her. Put his right hand over the coldest point, just above the weak breathing pouch. The ring pulsed. He hummed, low and steady, a single sustained note. The water in her lungs answered the song. It rose, it gathered, and he guided it upward with the melody, drawing it through her body and out of her mouth in a terrible, violent expulsion. She came awake gasping, retching, her body convulsing as it expelled the last of the sea. When she finally collapsed back, trembling but breathing, he removed his hand from her chest.

“Help me.”

He knew that one. “Help?” He shaped the word carefully. The human tongue always felt like speaking through something. “Why?”

She did not answer immediately, only lay there gasping, her body still wracked with shivers. When she finally spoke, her words came out raw. “I yearn,” she choked, “to keep my own heart a-beatin’.”

He considered this. Most things did. That was the fundamental argument of every living creature in every sea he had ever crossed, from the smallest bioluminescent drifter to the things that ate them, the will toward the next breath. He had fought for it himself, once or twice.

“Then it will be so.”

Her breathing eased. He listened to it change. He remained crouched beside her, watching the rise and fall of her chest, listening to the rhythm of her heartbeat. It was slowing now, stabilizing. Good.

“Your name?”

He startled; he’d assumed she was asleep. “Einarr.” The vowels were still awkward in his mouth. “You?”

“Sasi.” A pause. “Thank ye, Einarr.”

He scratched his cheek.

Not all sea dogs, it seemed, were without manners. He moved closer, hovered one hand just above her face, felt the warmth of her exhale against his palm. Steady. He pulled his hand back, crossing his legs awkwardly underneath him. He hummed once more, and felt the ring answer. Whatever this was, he would see it to its end.

How curious, that he found he wanted to.
