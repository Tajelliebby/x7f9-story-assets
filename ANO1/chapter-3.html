The sky had gone from indigo to something almost gold at its edges. Sasi laced the cord around her neck twice, the way she always had, so the stone would sit flat instead of swinging. The cord was thin and a little rough where it had been rebound, and she could feel the two halves of the crack against the dip in her throat.

She became aware that he was watching her.

Not the way the crew had watched her. Not with that particular kind of boredom that turned mean when it had nothing better to do. He was simply waiting. The way he had been since the moment she had woken to find him on that rock. She didn’t quite know what to do with that.

“Right,” she said, tying the cord behind her neck. “I’m ready.”

The sea was calmer now than it had been in the dark hours. Still heavy. Still swollen from last night, the waves rolling in broad grey hills that pushed against the shore with a low, restless moan. He turned toward the water without ceremony. He walked the way he spoke. Like he’d already decided where he was going before he’d started. She’d had captains who walked like that. They’d all been insufferable.

She wasn’t sure, yet, what this one was.

“I should warn ye,” she followed. The water at the shallows was cold. “I’m not exactly built for swimmin’.” She caught up to him in a few quick steps, ignoring her joints’ protest. “The crew reckoned I’d make a better anchor than a deckhand, way I sink.”

He didn’t look back. “Can you swim?”

“Aye, I can swim. Just…” she gestured vaguely at herself. “Not fast.”

“That will not matter.”

He waded in ahead of her, moving without hesitation, the heavy dark fabric of whatever he wore dragging at the surface before it soaked through and sank with him. He went further than she expected. Deeper. Then she lost him. The waves weren’t rough here but they were dark, and he had disappeared into it. She stopped. Chest-deep and shivering, the current pressing against her from two directions at once, something tugging low at her legs and something else pushing her sideways.

Then the water moved.

Not like a wave. Something below it, something passing through from underneath. She couldn’t see a thing, just the rolling surface and the pale horizon, but she felt it. Then he rose. He came up directly in front of her, and for a moment she genuinely did not understand what she was looking at.

He was still recognizably him. The breadth of his shoulders. The particular stillness of his face. But from the chest downward the water shifted with something vast and silver, something that caught even this thin dawn light and threw it back in ribbons. A tail. Long and deep-finned, the colour of a blade in the dark. Where his hips should have been the scales began, finer at the sides, larger toward the fin, each one edged in a cold shimmer that moved when he moved. She could see through parts of it. Not through, exactly. The way old glass was sometimes imperfect, the way a river went translucent at the edges. The shape of him beneath it, the current running over him.

She became aware that her mouth was open and closed it.

He looked no more concerned about this than he had about anything else. He reached for his forearm brace and worked something loose with the same care she’d seen earlier. This time it was a stone, pale as bone. He held it between two fingers.

“Be still,” he lifted it toward her forehead. She went still. He was very close. His face was level with hers and she had absolutely nowhere to look that wasn’t him. “Hold your breath,” he murmured in a low tone.

“Aye,” she said, and then, because her mouth did not always consult her before it spoke. “Been holdin’ me breath since before I could walk, me. Could do three ship-lengths by the time I were eight and I reckon I could stretch that further now, so ye needn’t worry on—”

The stone gripped. That was the only word for it. The stone itself, pulling gently inward as though it had located where it was meant to sit and decided to stay there. It was warm.

“Come.”

He sank below the surface, and took her with him.

Sasi had held her breath underwater before. She’d been thrown overboard three times in her life. The first was an accident. The second and third were less so. This was her first time being shipwrecked. She’d learned young that you had your lungs and that was all you had, and she’d gotten rather good at making them last.

Then he said, from very close, “Breathe.” She turned her head. The water was in her mouth. The water was everywhere. Breathing was how people drowned. A beat of silence stretched between them, and then the corner of his mouth shifted, just barely, when he lowered his head to face her. “You will not perish. Believe me, and breathe.”

Sasi breathed. She exhaled a rush of silver bubbles and pulled in another breath just to confirm she wasn’t dying, and she wasn’t, she was breathing, actually and genuinely breathing, and the feeling that came with it was the strangest thing she’d felt in twenty-two years of being alive.

She breathed a third time, deeply, and felt something in her chest go loose.

The dark water brightened, opening up around her into something vast and populated and entirely inhuman, and she forgot, for a moment, to feel anything except that.

Shiny silvers darted by, each small body catching the faint grey surface-light and throwing it back like flung coins. She spun slowly, watching them spiral. One came close, interested in her benefactor; it hovered by his pale hair that moved with the current like something alive, the pale strands that weren’t quite pale at all. When the scattered light hit them they went silver and then briefly blue. Sasi had never seen anything she didn’t have a name for before. She had a name for most things but not this.

His expression, when he caught her watching him, was impossible to read. But something in it shifted, barely. She looked away.

Don’t stare, she told herself sternly. He’s a person. Probably. Don’t stare.

The water shuddered. It started as a vibration. A low trembling that moved through the water and through her, through her ribs and her sternum and the backs of her teeth. Then sound, if you could call it that. A long, resonant moan that pressed against her from every direction at once. She spun.

A massive creature came out of the grey like a slow-moving cliff. It was longer than the Morgane had been. Longer by half again, maybe more. Its belly was pale and ridged, its back a dark mottled grey that disappeared into the murk above, its fins broader than she was tall. It moved with the unhurried certainty of something that had never once had reason to be afraid, and the pressure wave it pushed ahead of itself rolled over her like a soft wall, rocking her sideways.

Behind it, smaller. Two shapes. Smaller by comparison only. She watched them and felt something catch in her throat. The two small ones stayed close, tucking in against the vast shelter of the big one’s wake, following exactly where it led.

Her hand went to the cord at her neck without thinking.

She turned away before she could follow that thought further. The water blurred. She let it, instead looking toward the surface that was growing further and further away, the light coming through it in long tilted columns. Beautiful. Terribly beautiful. She hadn’t known the sea looked like this from the inside.

“We should proceed.” His voice carried strangely underwater. Lower than above. She felt the edges of it.

It wasn’t sharp, the way the captain’s orders had been sharp. It didn’t have that particular quality of someone who’d decided your time was less valuable than theirs.

“Aye,” she said. “Lead on.”

He turned downward and she followed. She kicked after him, and he rose to meet her, settling beside her with an ease that made her feel very human and very small. That was the first problem. The second was that the water seemed to have an opinion about where she should be, and that opinion was up. A constant gentle insistence pressing against her from below, her body rising no matter how hard she kicked against it. She tried angling herself downward more sharply. That helped for about three strokes before the current got underneath her again and started easing her back toward the surface.

All this ballast and ye still can’t stay down, she thought grimly. Crew always said they could use ye as an anchor. Turns out ye don’t float like one.

She hadn’t laughed at that when they’d said it, and she wasn’t laughing now. She just kicked harder and said nothing, because she was not going to be the reason they had to stop. He was further below now. She could see the shape of him in the darkening water, the slow movement of his tail. She was level with his tail now. The length of him was extraordinary. She’d known that on land in a general way, the way you knew a thing by looking, but here she understood it differently. She reached without quite meaning to, her fingers catching the edge of where scale met the paler skin above.

His hand closed around her upper arm. She barely had time to register it before the world rearranged itself. One moment she was struggling against the current alone, and the next her back was against his chest, his arm across her middle, and they were moving again. Downward, smoothly, without the slightest effort on his part.

Oh.

She was aware of her own heartbeat in a way she hadn’t been a moment ago. She was aware, distantly, that this was the closest she had been to another person in a very long time. He held her without commentary. The crew had commentary for everything.

The water was darker. She looked around, because that was easier than thinking about the arm around her. The silver schools were gone. The green-grey was becoming something darker, light from above had thinned to almost nothing, a faint grey suggestion. The cold had weight to it now, pressing inward. Small things drifted past in clusters, tiny and wriggling, catching what little glow remained and winking with it before the current took them. They were everywhere, she realised. Millions of them, suspended in the dark like animated dust. She watched them and felt herself relax by increments she hadn’t meant to give.

She was looking at one small cluster when the pressure changed. It came on slowly, then all at once. First a tightness in her ears, then across her chest, then something that felt horribly like her ribs bending inward, and she gasped before she could stop herself. The bubbles came out in a rush, and she opened her mouth to say something, anything—

Einarr’s head snapped toward her.

She hadn’t even reached his name. He was already moving, drawing her upward with one arm steady at her back, and she felt the pressure begin to give by increments, degree by degree, until her next breath came in full and the one after that came easier still.

“Thank—” she started.

“Be still,” he said. Not unkind. Stern.

She shut her mouth. He turned to face her fully and leaned in. Sasi flinched back before she caught herself; he also slowed. Her heartbeat moved faster than them both. His fingers found the back of her head, not a grip, a careful hold. She finally stopped squirming and he closed the distance. She felt the weight of him against the stone above her brow, watching his hair drift forward around them both so her gaze wouldn’t linger on dark eyes.

“I didn’t say hold your breath,” he hummed, his other hand finding hers in the dark of the water and closing around it.

“Einarr—”

Their lips met.

Her mind went quiet. Like a held breath. Like the moment before the wave comes and everything on the surface goes still. Then the sensation spread outward from the stone, thin and impossibly light, something settling over her skin from the outside in. Silk, she thought, and then not silk, something thinner than silk, something she could only feel and not name. It moved from her forehead down over her scalp and throat and shoulders, along her arms to her fingertips, all the way to the soles of her boots. All of her. And the pressure that had been bearing inward on her chest simply ceased. Not gradually. Gone. Like a hand, lifted.

He drew back. The hand that tangled in her chestnut hair brushed at her jaw. “Breathe.”

Her lungs complied, taking in that breath, even as her heart jack-hammered against its cage. Between them, briefly, something hung in the water. Several rings, faint and luminous, drifting outward in slow circles before they dissolved to nothing.

Sasi blinked.

“My apologies,” he murmured. “I neglected to wake the second purpose of the stone before we descended.” He paused, his jaw tightened faintly. “At the depth we had reached, without it, the ocean would have pressed inward until there was nothing of you left.”

She stared at him. “Nothin’?”

“Nothing.”

She turned that over carefully, and set it somewhere she could be properly horrified about it later. “And when ye…” she refused to say the word, “…when ye woke it. What was that.”

“The stone links us,” he said. “What the deep cannot do to me, it cannot do to you. We share a bond through it now. The ocean will not remember the difference between us.”

“Oh,” she said.

So. Not a kiss. A matter of angles and artifacts and the particular geometry of foreheads touching stones, and his face had been close to hers because foreheads required faces and faces were attached to heads and that was simply how the arrangement had worked out spatially. That was entirely the sum of it. Her first kiss was intact, unclaimed, still hers, she had not given it to anyone, and she was fine.

She was perfectly fine.

“Can ye read me mind?” she moved to her second biggest concern.

A longer pause than usual. “No,” he confirmed.

Thank the heavens.

“Right,” she said. She pressed the back of her hand to her cheek and found it considerably warmer than the surrounding water. “Right. I’ll try not to make a fuss again.”

He was looking at her. Not past her. Not at the middle distance, the way people looked when they’d decided she wasn’t worth the full attention. At her. “Fuss?”

“I won’t inconvenience ye to stop again.”

“You are no inconvenience.” She didn’t know what to do with that, so she nodded. “Are you harmed,” he said, after a moment, “in any way?”

Sasi opened her mouth. Closed it. She thought about how many times in the past few hours he had asked if she was hurt. She thought about how she couldn’t remember the last time anyone had asked before.

“I’m alright,” she said. Her voice came out even. She was rather proud of that.

“You are certain?”

“Aye. I told ye.”

He regarded her for a moment longer. Then he nodded, once, and turned downward, and the dark water opened up below them like a door.
