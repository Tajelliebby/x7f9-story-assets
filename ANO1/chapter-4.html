The dark came gradually, then all at once.

She had thought she understood dark. Twenty-two years on a ship taught you all its varieties: the thick black of a moonless sea, the smothering below-decks when the lantern went out, the specific dark behind your own eyes when you were trying very hard not to cry. She thought she knew what dark meant.

She had been wrong.

The colour had bled out by degrees, shifting through deep indigo, then a bruised and sunless grey, until there was nothing for the grey to hold onto and it gave up entirely. What replaced it was not darkness the way she knew darkness. It had a weight to it. A texture. It pressed against her eyes like something solid and she found herself blinking more than she needed to, some useless instinct reaching for a light that was not coming. But something else was.

She noticed it first as movement, slow and directionless, white specks drifting past from every angle. She watched one settle on the back of her hand and dissolve. Tiny. Weightless. There were thousands of them, tens of thousands, an endless quiet downfall filling the water from every direction she could see.

“What is this,” she asked.

He considered the question for a moment in his way. “The sea is always eating itself,” he said. “What falls is what remains. It feeds everything below.”

She looked up through the drift of it, watching the white specks moving through it like a living thing. Everything below, he’d said. Everything in this dark, sustained by what sifted down from a sun that never reached them.

She stored that away somewhere. It felt important, though she couldn’t have said why.

The cold had sharpened. Not the biting cold of surface water in a storm but something still and total, the cold of a place that had never once been warm. She was aware of it in her fingers first, then her jaw, then the slow creep of it working inward. His arm was across her middle and where his skin met her side through the soaked cloth of her dress was the only warmth she was entirely sure of. She tried not to think about that.

Something vast drifted into sight.

She smelled it before she saw it, which was a strange thing to notice underwater, but there was something in the water that shifted, something thick and trailing. Then the shape resolved out of the dark: long, pale, ribboning downward in curling fronds that had no visible end. The bell of it was broad as a longboat and faintly luminous, pulsing with a dim and rhythmic light, and its tentacles spread below it in all directions like a dropped net. Enormous. Slow. Ancient-looking, the way old things in the sea always looked, as though time moved differently for them.

She didn’t mean to grip his arm tighter. She did anyway.

“It drifts,” he said, before she could ask. “It does not hunt by pursuit. It waits.”

“For what?”

“For whatever comes close enough.”

She watched it turn in the current. The tentacles moved through the snow-fall in long, trailing arcs. There was something almost peaceful about it. Something that made her profoundly unwilling to be anywhere near those trailing fronds.

They gave it a wide berth without her having to ask.

Deeper still, and the world contracted. She became aware that she was tracking him more by sensation than sight: the shift of water when his tail moved, the steadiness of his chest against her shoulder, the slow and certain rhythm of his heart. Her own heart was doing something entirely different. She was aware of this and chose not to examine it.

Then the light came.

A single point of it, far below and slightly to her left. Blue-white. Lovely. She watched it drift and bob and for one foolish moment thought it was a lantern someone had lost, sinking slowly toward the dark floor of the sea. She reached for it.

His hand closed around her wrist before she got there.

The light moved toward her fingers anyway.

She saw the teeth first. Then the shape behind the teeth: a body, small and deeply ugly, all jaw and hollow eyes, the luminous lure swinging from its brow like a fishing line. It came at her fingers with extraordinary intent for something barely longer than her hand, and then it was not coming at her fingers anymore because it was in his palm, snapping with tremendous enthusiasm at something it was not going to be able to bite through.

Sasi made a sound she would not be describing to anyone.

Her arms went around his neck. Her legs came up. Some part of her brain was aware this was not a measured response to the situation and the rest of her brain did not care at all.

“It is small,” he said. Patient.

“It has got a lot of teeth for something small,” she said, from somewhere near his shoulder.

“It does.” He held it up. The lure swung. The tiny creature thrashed with a fury completely disproportionate to its size. “It will wait as long as it must,” he said, almost to himself. “The light is all it has. But it has learned to make that enough.”

She looked at it then, properly. The hollow eyes that couldn’t see. The light that bobbed and swayed on its line, making the dark around it seem darker by comparison. Patient little monster. She thought about that for a moment.

“Let it go,” she said.

He did. They both watched it descend, its lure dimming, until it vanished.

She became aware that she was still wrapped around him like particularly anxious rigging. She unwrapped herself with as much dignity as she could locate and settled back against his chest, which was where she had apparently decided she lived now.

She kept her expression very calm. She was fine.

Her heartbeat was not consulting her on this matter.

The dark thickened further and further until it was no longer something her eyes could attempt. This was not night. Night had shapes in it, suggestions, the outline of a sail against stars. This was the absence of light as a fundamental condition, as a thing the water had decided would simply be true here and had been true long before anyone thought to argue. She could not see her own hands.

She felt him shift. His arm tightened around her once. Then he raised his free hand and she felt, rather than saw, the ring on his finger come alive: a low vibration, a warmth against her side, a faint violet pulse that she caught at the edge of her vision. He pressed his hand forward into the dark.

The dark rippled.

She had not known dark could do that. It moved like disturbed water, but it was not water, or it was not only water. It pulled away from his hand in rings, the way a still pond moved when you dropped something into it, and where it moved she could see for just a moment the texture of what was on the other side. Then the rippling reached her.

The world pressed in.

It was not the crushing pressure from before; the membrane held. But there was a heaviness that settled over her all at once, something that had nothing to do with weight and everything to do with depth, the way the air felt before a storm but magnified a thousandfold. Her ears rang with a sound below sound. Her stomach turned over once, sharply, and she pressed her lips together and breathed through her nose and thought about something ordinary, the smell of a rope, the weight of a mop handle, the grain of a deck plank under her bare feet.

Then the heaviness lifted.

The dark gave way.

It did not vanish all at once. It peeled back by degrees, like a curtain drawn aside, and what came through first was colour: cold blue, ghostly green, a deep and sourceless violet glowing from somewhere far below. Then shapes. Spires. Structures that rose out of the dark floor of the sea in spiraling columns, black as the water itself but edged in light, covered in pale lattice-work so fine it looked like something spun rather than built. They reached upward with no apparent concern for stopping. She could see the light moving through them, the glow passing from level to level in pulses, and between them the water shimmered with falling white.

The snow was still falling here. It always fell here. She understood suddenly that it always had.

She forgot to breathe.

He was watching her. She could tell by the angle of him without looking, his attention redirected from the city to her face. She kept her eyes on the spires.

“Djúp-Vaggr,” he said.

His voice carried something in it. Something that was not quite pride and not quite tenderness and was perhaps the version of those things that his particular kind of stillness allowed. She had only known him a handful of hours but she already understood that this was not a small thing he was offering.

She nodded once, very precisely, because she did not trust what her voice would do.

He descended. Not quickly. He took his time in a way that she was already beginning to recognise as deliberate, tail-first, his pace unhurried, giving her the full slow unwrapping of it. She watched the spires grow around them, taller and taller, the pale frost-like latticing catching the crystal-light and throwing it back in fragments. The streets below were dark stone, polished mirror-smooth, reflecting the glow of the lamps that lined them in what would have been rows if the roads had run straight, but they didn’t. They curved. They spiraled. They followed the walls of the caldera as though they had grown from it naturally rather than been laid by anyone’s hand.

Two figures appeared at what she understood to be an entrance.

They were large. Larger than the sailors she’d known, and she’d known large sailors. Their armor was pale and architectural, their eyes closed and yet they tracked her all the same, heads turning fractionally as Einarr brought them down to the street-level. They made a sound she felt in her back teeth. He answered it. They moved aside.

She looked at them out of the corner of her eye as they passed. They did not look back. She was glad about that. She was not going to tell anyone that she was glad about that.

Then the city swallowed them.

She had been to ports. She had not been to ports, exactly; she had seen them from the rail of the Morgane while her father conducted his business and the crew moved freely and she stayed where she was put. But she had seen them well enough to know the quality of a place that moved. The particular hum of commerce, the way sound accumulated in confined spaces when many people were going about their lives at once.

This was that. Translated into something entirely other.

The sounds first: not voices as she understood voices, but something clicking and tonal, rhythms that overlapped and wove around each other, occasional low resonant tones that moved through the water and through her chest in waves. The clicking was fast and deliberate and everywhere. She couldn’t follow any of it. She wasn’t sure her ears were built to follow it. But she understood, the way you understood a port even when everyone in it was speaking a language you didn’t know, that information was moving.

Then the people.

They filled the space the way crowds always filled space, flowing around obstacles, gathering in clusters, dispersing. But they were nothing like any crowd she had ever imagined. Some of them caught what little light there was and threw it back from their skin, luminous where her eye expected to find shadow. Some of them were dark as the water, nearly invisible until they moved, and then the movement itself caught the light in scales and fragments. Their tails were every kind of thing: broad-finned and narrow, rippling and rigid, pale and deep-dark.

A small creature pottered past on stubby fins, its lure dimmed to almost nothing, following at the heels of a figure who did not look down. She tracked it until it disappeared into the crowd.

Stalls lined the thoroughfare, carved from something so dark it absorbed the crystal-light rather than reflecting it. Things were displayed on them that she had no names for, bright and strange, winking violet and green in the dimness. Someone was playing music, or something that functioned as music, a low resonant tone that had a melody in it if you waited for it to repeat.

She craned her neck. She wanted more time.

He turned down a corridor away from all of it.

“Oh, come on,” she said, before she could stop herself.

He glanced back at her. Something in his expression was almost amused.

She subsided. She kept looking back over her shoulder for as long as the corridor allowed her a view, watching the light of the market recede. The sounds softened by degrees, the clicking and the tonal calls and the low music muffling through the dark stone walls until it was only a suggestion, then nothing.

The corridor opened into a courtyard.

She had no better word for it, though it was bigger than any courtyard she had seen drawings of, rising high enough that the top of it vanished into dark. The structures around it were immense: she could see the architecture better here, where there was space to look. The walls were black stone and pale lattice and the occasional panel of something clear as old glass through which the crystal-light moved. A gate stood ahead of them, higher than it had any reason to be, and at the gate were more figures like the two at the entrance.

She was beginning to understand that this was a large building.

She was beginning to understand other things as well, slower and with more resistance.

He stopped before the gate. Turned to her, and put a hand on her shoulder, and said, low and careful: “Stay behind me.”

“Aye,” she said.

She released his neck and planted her feet and immediately began to drift upward.

Right. Water. She had almost forgotten, which was frankly embarrassing.

His arm came back around her without comment. He moved her behind him with the same unhurried efficiency he brought to everything, keeping her there with one hand while he turned to face the gate. She could feel his chest expand. Then the sound came: low, resonant, rolling outward from him like something structural, less a voice than a frequency, a thing the water transmitted as much as the air. She felt it in her ribs and in her jaw and in the stone at her forehead and it kept going, carrying further than she could track.

A response came from the other side of the gate. Lower still. It moved through her differently, heavier, and she had the instinctive impression of age. Of something very old.

The gate opened. Sound travelled through the water differently than through air but she heard it still, a long deep groan that arrived in her ears slower than she expected, lower in pitch than stone and metal had any right to produce.

They went through.

The room beyond was long and high-ceilinged and lit from the walls in a cold blue that made everything look like the moment before dawn. She registered the space and the light and then she registered the people in it, ranged around a central table in various states of stillness, and then she registered the two at the far end and everything else became peripheral.

The man in the carved seat.

He was the largest person she had ever seen and he looked like he had been made to be looked at, the way certain storms looked, the way certain rocks at the waterline looked, like a thing that had decided on permanence a long time ago and had never revisited the question. His hair was dark and silver and his face had the particular quality of something that had made a great many decisions and had been right about most of them. His tail where it curved back from the seat was immense, deep-finned, patterned in dark scales that caught the light in cold fragments.

She did not realise, immediately, that this was the source of the lower resonance from the gate.

The woman beside him was an entirely different proposition. Warm where he was cold. Her hair was arranged with something that caught the crystal-light in red and deep amber, and her tail was golden along the spine in a way that threw a faint warm light across the floor of the room. She was watching Sasi with an expression that was attentive and kind and contained something that Sasi did not know how to categorise, a specific quality of consideration, the way you looked at something you were trying to understand because you thought it might matter.

Einarr released her wrist and lifted her hand. The hand he had cut. He held it up without speaking.

Someone else spoke instead.

A different voice, sharp and objecting, from somewhere to her left. She looked toward it. Couldn’t see past the breadth of Einarr’s shoulders. She craned slightly. He was built, she was noticing, the way cliffs were built, in that his shoulder was in fact directly level with her eye and she had a very thorough view of his arm and the muscle in it and very little view of anything else. She tried to see past it. The arm flexed slightly as he turned in response to whatever was being said, and she stopped trying and looked at the arm instead, which was at least something to look at while she waited for things to make sense.

Another voice cut across the first. This one from the direction of the thrones. It had the quality of something that had decided the first voice was done now.

Then it continued, and it said, in words she understood, clear and unhurried:

“Human child. Come forward.”

She peered around Einarr’s shoulder. He was already looking at her. The corner of his mouth had done that thing it did, and she recognised it well enough now to know that he was entertained, which was deeply unfair of him.

She came around in front of him, into the open space of the room, and looked at the two in the carved seats properly for the first time.

The woman was smiling softly. Sasi felt something in her chest go still, the particular stillness of something that had been braced and found nothing to brace against. She looked between the woman and Einarr. Then back. Then at Einarr again.

There was something about the line of the jaw. The particular set of the expression.

“My son tells me,” the woman said, warm and measured, “that he wishes you to join us.”

Sasi’s mind stopped. “Son?”

The woman’s eyes curved. “I see he did not mention it.”

“Tell me what?” Sasi turned to look at him fully.

From around the room, small sounds. Exhaled breath, soft clicks, something that might have been laughter in a language she didn’t have. She was aware of the room’s attention and she was aware of her heartbeat, which was not behaving, and she pressed her palm to her sternum once before she caught herself.

The woman rose. Even with her tail trailing behind her she was immense, moving with the unhurried grace of someone who had never once had to wonder if they belonged in a space. She crossed toward Sasi and the warmth of her presence preceded her like the pockets of warm water in the corridors, inexplicable and real.

“I am Herdis,” she said. “Queen of these waters.” She gestured, the way you indicated something very large that was all around you. “Beside me, my husband and your king. Halvdan.” And then, as though it were the most ordinary thing in the world to say: “And behind you stands our third child. Einarr of Clan Hval-Bein. Prince of this kingdom.”

The room was very quiet. Sasi turned around.

He was not looking away. He met her gaze with that perfect, untroubled composure and the corner of his mouth was still doing what it was doing, that not-quite-smile she had been collecting examples of for the past few hours.

A prince. She had followed a prince into the sea. She had wept in front of a prince. She had clung to a prince’s neck when a small fish frightened her and called him a stranger and offered him service on a black sand beach at dawn like an absolute fool and he had said nothing, he had said not one word about any of it, and he was standing there now looking at her like he was watching something very interesting unfold and she could not decide if she wanted to laugh or sink directly into the floor of the ocean and wait there until everyone had forgotten she existed.

She opted for a third choice, which was to turn back around and direct her attention to the king.

He was watching her with eyes that asked questions without asking them. When he spoke it moved through the water and through her and her heartbeat stumbled and recovered.

“Have you lost your tongue, little human?”

“No, sir,” she said. Very steady. Hands clasped in front of her, skirt fabric twisted tight between her fingers where no one could see. “I have it still.”

Something moved at the edges of the room. That same collection of small sounds.

The king regarded her for a long moment. “Then. The proposition before you. Humanity surrendered. A place made for you among us.” His eyes did not leave her. “Is this agreeable to you.”

She thought about the Morgane. The deck planks under her bare feet, which were the only floors she had ever known. Her father’s back, always turned. The particular smell of blood and saltwater and unwashed men that had been the smell of her entire life. She thought about the stone at her throat. Its two halves, held by a cord that had been cut and rebound by someone who had pulled it from the sea for her.

She had not much of a life to surrender.

“Aye, sir,” she said, and meant it. “There weren’t much to bein’ human, if I’m honest.”

The room breathed.

The king looked at her for one more long moment. Then he looked at Einarr, something passing between them that she couldn’t read.

“Well then,” the Queen smiled, “You should choose your guardian.”
