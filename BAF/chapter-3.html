The night sky stretched vast and ink-black over Kabukicho, stars drowned beneath the neon bleeding up from Shinjuku’s streets. Buildings crowded together below, their lit windows forming a jagged horizon around the dark. Somewhere in that tangle of concrete and glass, a ramen shop’s red lantern swayed in the evening breeze.

The alley behind it smelled of fryer grease and old cigarette smoke.

Ryuji leaned against the damp brick wall, tucked out of sight where the main street couldn’t reach him. The cigarette between his lips glowed orange as he inhaled, smoke curling past his face. He checked his watch, the mechanical kind, his oyabun’s gift from three years back, then slid his hand into his pocket. Long black hair fell loose around his shoulders tonight, still damp from the shower he’d rushed through an hour ago.

A shadow crossed the mouth of the alley.

His spine straightened. The cigarette stayed between his lips as he leaned forward, eyes tracking the figure that passed: cheap suit, wrinkled; briefcase clutched too tight; thinning hair plastered to a sweaty forehead despite the October chill.

Hiroto.

The cigarette hit the ground. Ryuji crushed it under his heel and pushed off the wall, his stride lengthening into something that made passersby step aside without quite knowing why. Hiroto’s shoulders hunched, head down, moving fast, like a man who believed speed could outrun his problems.

It couldn’t.

Ryuji’s hand shot out and fisted in the man’s collar, yanking him sideways into the alley with enough force that his briefcase swung wild. Papers scattered across the damp pavement.

“Yoshida-san, please…” Hiroto’s voice cracked. His hands came up defensively. “I just need more time. I swear I can…”

“Nine days.” Ryuji’s voice came out flat, cold. He shoved Hiroto against the wall hard enough that the man’s teeth clicked together. “You already had more time. Oyabun was patient. I told him I’d handle it.”

“I know, I know, but my daughter, she’s…”

“Don’t.” Ryuji’s grip tightened on the man’s jacket.

Hiroto’s eyes were wet now, his face pale in the light filtering in from the street. The briefcase had popped open when it hit the ground: expense reports and what looked like betting slips, soaking in a puddle. Ryuji drew his fist back, weight shifting into his hips.

“Oh my god!”

The voice cut through the tension like a bell, high and soft and entirely out of place in a back alley where violence was about to happen. Ryuji froze, fist still cocked, and looked over his shoulder. She stood maybe ten feet away, one hand pressed to her chest as though she’d stumbled on a car accident. Expensive coat, designer bag, the kind of put-together that didn’t belong in Kabukicho after dark. Porcelain skin caught the distant neon; ice-blue eyes went wide, fixed on him.

Then she moved. Heels clicked across the pavement as she closed the distance, small fingers wrapping around his wrist. “Ryu, what’s going on?” Her voice carried that concerned sweetness that made men want to explain themselves, to justify. “What’s wrong?”

Hiroto’s eyes went wider. “Wait. Park Hae-eun?” He said her name like she was royalty, like he couldn’t quite believe it. “Miss Park, what are you doing in a place like…”

“Because my boyfriend is here duh.” She smiled and slid her hand down to lace her fingers with Ryuji’s. The red bands on both their left pinkies touched. “I’m so sorry about this. He has a bit of a temper, gets into fights sometimes. But he’s really a good person. Please, I hope you can forgive him.”

Hiroto’s mouth opened, eyes darting between their joined hands, the matching bands, Ryuji’s face. “Miss Park, you don’t understand. This man is… he’s ya…”

Ryuji’s eyes narrowed, the look carrying every unspoken threat he’d learned in fifteen years of this life: shut your mouth before I shut it for you. Hiroto’s words died in his throat. He backed away, stumbling over his own feet as he fled down the alley toward the street. They stood there until his footsteps faded into the ambient noise of Shinjuku at night: car horns, distant karaoke, the hum of a thousand neon signs.

Haeun let out a small hmph and dropped his hand, settling her own on her hips. The concerned civilian act evaporated like it had never existed. She turned to face him fully, head tilted. “What was that about?”

“Hiroto owes us money.” Ryuji’s gaze stayed on the mouth of the alley. “Five hundred thousand yen. Nine days overdue.”

“Oh.” She paused, expression shifting thoughtful. “Well, if it’s just money, what if I paid his debt?”

“Can’t do that.” He finally looked at her, catching the way streetlight painted blue highlights in her black hair. “We have rules. Protocols. Can’t just…”

“Oh.” She drew the word out, turning to look the same direction Hiroto had gone, eyes unfocusing slightly, that faraway look she got when she was reading someone’s surface thoughts. “But he’s planning to run tonight. Around eleven, I think. Not taking his wife and kid.”

Ryuji’s head snapped toward her so fast something in his neck protested. Surprise, then disbelief, then something dark and dangerous settling in, the kind that made the alley feel ten degrees colder. “Is that so?”

She giggled, bright and genuine, like they were sharing an inside joke instead of a man’s impending doom. “Yeah. So… exercise after dinner?”

A slow smile spread across his face, the kind that never reached his eyes when he wore it for work. But looking at her, something in it softened at the edges. “You’re fucking perfect. Let’s eat.”

The ramen shop’s interior was warm, almost too warm after the October chill. Condensation fogged the windows; the air smelled of pork broth and garlic. The owner waved them toward a table by the window without asking what they wanted. They’d been coming here long enough that the old man knew their order by heart, which said something about either their loyalty or their lack of imagination.

An elderly couple sat three tables over, both with red bands on their left pinkies. The woman was feeding her husband a bite of chashu, laughing as he tried to talk around the food. They had that comfortable way of existing in each other’s space that came from decades together.

The old woman caught Haeun’s eye and smiled, nodding toward their matched bands. “Still in the honeymoon phase, I see.”

Her husband swallowed his food and grinned. “We were like that once, weren’t we?”

“Were? Who says we stopped?”

Haeun smiled back, the kind that actually reached her eyes. Ryuji felt that familiar tug in his chest, the one that still surprised him six months in, as though his body hadn’t quite accepted this was real. Their ramen arrived: two bowls of tonkotsu, the broth so rich it coated the spoon. Haeun’s face lit up.

“Pork!” She picked up her chopsticks, already reaching for the thick slices of chashu. “You’re flirting with me.”

“Am I?” He broke his chopsticks apart, stirring through the noodles.

“Mm.” She took a bite, eyes closing in appreciation. “You know, the livers here are so different from back home.”

He paused mid-slurp, noodles dangling. “Yeah? How so?”

“Not fatty enough.” She hummed. “Korean diet makes them different. Leaner. Japanese livers are good, but…” Her gaze went distant, considering. “Maybe we should visit America for our anniversary?”

Ryuji set down his chopsticks, thumb tracing over the red band on her pinkie. “Fatty American livers. Fast food culture.”

“Exactly!” Her eyes sparkled. “So much marbling.”

He pulled out his phone, already opening a browser. “When were you thinking? Spring? Summer?”

“Spring. Cherry blossoms here, then we fly out.” She leaned closer, peering at his screen. “Start in the West maybe? I’ve heard great things about West Virginia.”

“Biscuits and Gravy?” He laughed, scrolling. “Okay, yea, I guess. Look, this place has a whole tour…”

She snorted into her broth. “Romantic.”

A young woman approached their table, nervous energy radiating off her. No band on her left pinkie. “Um, excuse me?” She twisted her hands together. “Park Hae-eun-ssi?”

Haeun’s entire demeanor shifted, spine straightening, smile softening into something gracious and approachable. “Yes?”

“I’m such a huge fan! Your father’s company, the charity work you do…” The girl’s words tumbled over each other. “Could I get a selfie?” Then her eyes caught on the red band. “Oh! Oh my god, you found your soulmate! Congratulations!”

“Thank you.” Haeun stood, already angling for the photo. “Yes, very recently.”

The girl’s friends materialized, three of them clustering around for pictures, chattering about fashion, some new skincare line Park Industries was launching, an interview Haeun had given last month about women in business.

Ryuji watched. He couldn’t help it: the way she moved between personas like changing clothes, the way she tucked her hair behind her ear because it photographed better that way, the little laugh she gave that sounded genuine even though he knew it wasn’t. He’d once told her she should’ve been an actress. She’d told him she basically was. One of the friends noticed him staring and elbowed another girl, whispering something that made them both giggle and blush.

“You’re so lucky,” the first fan said to Haeun, but she was looking at Ryuji. “You two look so happy together.”

“I know.” His voice came out softer than intended.

Haeun glanced at him, something flickering across her face too fast to name, making the red string between their pinkies pulse with a warmth only they could feel. “Ryu, stop.” She whined, cheeks pink. “You’re embarrassing me.”

“Stop what?” He picked up his chopsticks again. “I’m just sitting here.”

The fans left with their photos, one sighing dramatically about hoping to meet her own soulmate soon. The elderly couple had paid their bill and shuffled out while Haeun was distracted, the woman giving Ryuji a knowing wink on her way past.

Haeun sat back down and immediately flagged the waiter. “Chicken katsu please, extra vegetables. To go.”

Ryuji added an order for chocolate lava cake and two whiskeys. When she raised an eyebrow, he shrugged. “What? I have a sweet tooth.”

“I’m baking for you sometime.” She said it like a threat and a promise. “You won’t need to order dessert anymore.”

“Looking forward to it. Try not to poison me, I’ve got a busy night.”

The food came. Haeun handled the bottles while he handled the cake. When the bill came, she pressed more yen than necessary into his hand. “For the trouble,” she said, nodding toward where they’d caused a small scene with the fans.

“Thank you, Park-san. Please come again.”

Outside, Haeun stretched, patting her stomach. “I’m so full. That was perfect.”

“Good.” He took her hand, their pinkies automatically linking. “Ready for exercise?”

She stopped mid-stretch. “Oh god, I forgot about my babysitters.”

“We’ll lose them first. Come on.”

His motorcycle was parked two blocks over, tucked between a pachinko parlor and a convenience store. Haeun climbed on behind him without hesitation, arms wrapping around his waist as he kicked the engine to life, chin resting on his shoulder. He felt more than heard her contented sigh.

They stopped at her place, a high-rise in Roppongi that cost more per month than most people made in a year. The bodyguards were exactly where they always were: one in the lobby, one in a car outside. Haeun waved cheerfully as they passed, calling out something about being exhausted and going straight to bed.

Inside, she dropped the act. They went up to her floor, into her apartment, straight through to her bedroom. The window opened silently; she’d oiled the hinges months ago for exactly this purpose. Fire escape down, around the building through the back alley, out to where Ryuji had circled back with the bike.

The bodyguards never noticed. To be fair, they were paid to watch the door, not the woman using her window like a fire drill.

“Where to?” He twisted to look at her over his shoulder.

“Haneda. But not the airport.” She adjusted her grip on him. “The docks.”

The river stretched out black and glittering under the lights from the opposite bank. The docks weren’t busy at this hour, but they weren’t empty either. Couples walked hand in hand, a few fishermen tried their luck, someone played guitar badly near one of the bench areas. Fairy lights strung between the lamp posts gave the whole place a soft, romantic glow that didn’t quite match the industrial smell of diesel and river water.

Haeun squeezed his waist. “Oh, we should come here sometime. Just to walk and look.”

“Yeah?” He killed the engine, put down the kickstand. “We can do that. After.”

“After exercise.” She climbed off, immediately scanning the area. “Perfect way to wind down.”

It took her less than three minutes to spot Hiroto. He sat on a bench near a small private dock, a duffel bag at his feet, his briefcase clutched on his lap like a life preserver. Waiting for his ride out, probably. Some fishing boat captain paid under the table to take him south, away from Tokyo and the debts he’d left behind.

“There.” Haeun pointed, then turned to Ryuji with a grin. “Let me handle this?”

“You sure?”

“Yeah, I got it.” She held out her designer clutch. “Hold my purse?”

He took it without hesitation, sliding the strap over his shoulder and tucking the small bag under his arm. It looked absurd against his frame, a man built like a warning label carrying something that matched her shoes. She kissed his cheek, quick and casual, then practically skipped toward Hiroto. Her heels clicked against the concrete.

Ryuji stayed back, leaning against the motorcycle. Close enough to intervene if needed, far enough to let her work. The red string between their pinkies stretched taut as she moved away, glowing softly in his peripheral vision. He touched his band absently, feeling the slight vibration through their connection.

Haeun’s voice carried back to him, bright with false surprise. “Oh my god! Hiroto-san! Fancy meeting you here!”

Hiroto jumped like she’d shot him. “Miss Park! What are you…” His eyes darted around, looking for exits, for witnesses, for anything that might save him.

“What a coincidence!” She settled onto the bench next to him like old friends catching up. “Are you traveling? Oh, don’t worry, my boyfriend isn’t with me.” A little rueful face, apologizing for earlier. “Sorry about what happened. He can get rough sometimes, but he means well.”

“Miss Park.” Hiroto was sweating despite the cool air. “I mean this respectfully, but you should stay away from that man. He’s dangerous. A criminal.”

“A criminal?” She tilted her head. Ryuji watched the exact moment her shoulders tensed, and decided it was time to get closer. “Why? Because he collects debts?”

“He’s yakuza! He nearly killed me!” Hiroto’s voice pitched higher. “Your soulmate is… look, I saw your bands, you’re bonded. But that doesn’t mean you have to stay. You can break it off. Find someone better!”

“Break it off?” Haeun’s smile didn’t waver, but something in her tone went colder. “Why would I do that?”

“Because he’s…” Hiroto gestured helplessly. “People like that don’t deserve soulmates!”

“Hmm.” She looked out at the water, considering. “So men who borrow from criminals to feed a gambling habit aren’t bad men? Only the ones who collect?”

Hiroto’s mouth opened. Nothing came out.

“And what about men who leave their wives and children in the middle of the night?” She turned back to him, eyes gone just slightly wrong, too bright, too focused. “While fleeing debts they promised to pay? Are those good men?”

“How did you…”

“You wear a wedding ring.” Haeun gestured at his bare pinkie. “But you don’t have a band. Did you even bother meeting her properly, or was she just convenient?”

Hiroto stood so fast his briefcase fell off his lap. “I don’t have to listen to this! You don’t know anything about…”

“She asked you a question.” Ryuji’s voice came from directly behind him. Hiroto stumbled backward. Ryuji caught him by the elbow, almost gentle, like steadying someone who’d tripped, and guided him back down onto the bench. “There we go.” Ryuji kept his hand on Hiroto’s shoulder, not squeezing, not threatening, just there. “Now. Answer her question.”

Hiroto’s eyes ping-ponged between them. “You’re both insane! Soulmates don’t… people don’t…”

“Don’t what?” Ryuji’s voice stayed level, almost conversational. “Love each other? Accept each other?”

Haeun stood, moving to stand next to Ryuji, their pinkies hooking together automatically, bands touching. “We’re perfect for each other. The universe said so.”

“That’s not…” Hiroto was shaking. “That’s not how it works!”

“It’s exactly how it works.” Haeun smiled down at him. “Hiroto-san, why don’t you come with us? We just want to talk.”

She crouched to his eye level. Hiroto’s pupils dilated immediately, his whole body going slack like someone had cut his strings. When she stood and offered her hand, he took it without hesitation.

They walked him toward the warehouse district, away from the fairy lights and the couples and the bad guitar player. Hiroto followed like a sleepwalker. The warehouse was one of dozens in this part of the port, abandoned when the shipping company went under three years ago, never torn down because the real estate remained tied up in legal disputes. Ryuji had used it twice before. Good acoustics, no neighbors. The local homeless population knew better than to bed down anywhere that smelled like yakuza territory.

One bare bulb hung from the ceiling, swinging slightly in the draft from a broken window. Concrete floor, water damage creeping up the walls, a single metal folding chair Ryuji had dragged in from god knew where.

Hiroto sat in it, blinked, looked around like he’d just woken up. “W-where…” His voice cracked. “What do you want from me?”

Ryuji and Haeun stood about ten feet away near the door, her hand out, examining her nails in the dim light, his free hand scrolling through his phone while his other stayed linked with hers.

“So I was thinking,” Haeun said, as though she were picking up the middle of a conversation, “for the anniversary trip, since where’s staying for the flowers, maybe we finally book that hotel in Shibuya. The one Aniki wouldn’t shut up about.”

Ryuji made a considering noise, thumb already moving over his phone screen. “The rooftop one?”

“That’s the one.” She perked up, leaning in to see his screen. “He said the view alone was worth it.”

“Four stars.” He found the listing, tilted the phone toward her. “Look, they’ve got a whole couples package…”

“W-what do you want from me?” Hiroto’s voice pitched panicky.

They both paused, turned to look at him. “Oh.” Haeun blinked. “Right. You.”

“Please, I have a family. I can get you money. I know people…”

“We don’t want money.” Ryuji’s tone was bored.

“Is this what soulmates do?” Hiroto’s laugh was half-hysterical. “Kill people together?”

“Only the ones who deserve it!” Haeun said it cheerfully, like discussing their favorite restaurant.

“People know I’m here! If I don’t check in…”

“No they don’t.” She giggled. “You were sneaking away, remember?”

“My daughter!” Hiroto’s eyes were wet. “She’s only eight. She needs her father…”

“Should’ve thought of that before you gambled away her college fund and tried to run.” Ryuji’s voice went flat. “And abandoned your wife. Who actually loves you, even without a soulmate bond.”

“That hotel you mentioned earlier.” Hiroto’s words tumbled over each other, desperate. “The one in Shibuya? It’s a ripoff. Four stars but overpriced, doesn’t even include breakfast.”

Ryuji and Haeun stopped talking. Both turned to face him fully. “…really?” Haeun’s head tilted.

“Y-yes!” Hiroto seized on their attention like a lifeline. “My cousin stayed there last month, said the AC barely works and they charge extra for WiFi. There’s a better place in Yokohama, same price range…”

“Yokohama?” Ryuji’s tone shifted, interested now.

“Traditional ryokan. Amazing service. Includes dinner and breakfast, hot spring on-site.” Hiroto was talking faster, sensing he’d found something. “Perfect for soulmate couples. They have couple packages. Very romantic.”

“Didn’t your brother recommend the Shibuya place?” Haeun looked at Ryuji.

“Yeah, but Aniki has shit taste. Remember the restaurant incident?”

“Oh god, the fish.” Haeun laughed, genuine and bright. “Besides, he doesn’t even have a soulmate. What does he know about romantic getaways?”

Hiroto kept going, words spilling out now: restaurant recommendations, hotel reviews, which tourist traps to avoid in Osaka, the best time to visit Kyoto, where to get authentic street food in Fukuoka. They listened, asked follow-up questions, made notes in their phones. Haeun absently played with Ryuji’s pinkie, the red string wrapping around both their fingers as she suggested adding Hiroshima to the itinerary.

Hiroto started to relax, his shoulders coming down from where they’d been hunched near his ears, breathing evening out. He was helping them plan a vacation. They were treating him like a travel agent, not a victim. Maybe if he was useful enough, if he gave them enough good information, they’d let him go.

The conversation wound down. Silence settled over the warehouse, broken only by the drip of water somewhere in the back. Hiroto stood. “Well, uh, if that’s everything…” He turned toward the door, took two steps.

“Babe.” Ryuji’s voice was casual, almost lazy. “Dessert’s trying to leave.”

Haeun’s head snapped up. “Ooh, yes!”

Hiroto froze, turned slowly.

Haeun was smiling, too wide, too many sharp edges in an expression that should have been warm. Her eyes flashed gold and stayed gold, pupils stretched vertical like a cat’s. Or a fox’s.

“W-what…” Hiroto backed up until his shoulders hit the wall.

The glamour dropped. Not dramatic, no smoke, no sound effects. Just one second she was Haeun Park, delicate and beautiful and human, and the next, she wasn’t. Nine tails unfurled behind her, translucent and glowing faintly blue in the dim warehouse. Fox ears pushed up through her black hair. Her fingers elongated, nails sharpening into claws, fangs sliding down over her lower lip. She didn’t look human anymore, but she was still beautiful in a way that made your brain scream at you to run.

The red band stayed on her pinkie. The only part of her that didn’t change.

“You’re… you’re a MONSTER!” Hiroto’s scream echoed off the concrete walls. “How can you have a soulmate?! That’s not… soulmates don’t…”

Haeun stalked forward, each step predatory and unhurried. “Don’t what?”

“Monsters can’t have soulmates! The universe wouldn’t…” Hiroto tried to press himself through the wall and failed.

She leaned in close, so close her breath ghosted across his face. “Want to know a secret?” Her voice dropped to a whisper. “We do. And he’s mine.”

Ryuji stayed by the door, arms crossed, watching her, not the violence. Fifteen years in this life had burned the shock out of him for one, but never for the other: the way her tails moved independently, balancing her as she pinned Hiroto with supernatural strength; the way her whole body flowed like water, inhuman grace that had fascinated him since the first time he’d seen it.

Hiroto’s screams cut off abruptly.

Ryuji absently touched his red band. The string glowed brighter in his vision. She was fully in her element right now, thriving. “Hey.” His voice carried across the warehouse. “You were looking thin lately. I was worried.”

“Mm?” Haeun’s response was muffled. “I’m fine! Last week’s meal tided me over.”

“Still.” He pushed off the wall, taking a few steps closer. “You need to eat regularly. Can’t have you wasting away.”

“Aw, babe.”

Something rolled across the concrete: Hiroto’s arm, severed at the elbow, coming to rest against Ryuji’s boot. He bent down, picked it up and walked over to where Haeun crouched over what was left of Hiroto.

He held it out. “Don’t be picky. Eat everything.”

She looked up at him, face covered in blood, eyes still gold, and giggled. “Yes, sir.”

The string between them pulsed warm and bright as she took the arm from his hand, their fingers brushing, blood-slick and sticky, neither of them caring. She finished her meal methodically, efficiently. By the time she was done, her appearance had started to fade back: tails retracting, ears shrinking, fangs sliding back up. The gold bled out of her eyes until they were ice-blue again. By the end, she looked like delicate Hae-eun Park again, just with blood on her face and clothes, and, somehow, not a single drop on the red band.

Ryuji offered her his handkerchief, monogrammed, a gift from his oyabun. She took it and wiped her mouth with the same careful delicacy she used fixing her lipstick. Their pinkies touched as she handed the cloth back, the string glowing warm between them, wrapping around both their hands like an embrace.

“Better?” he asked.

“Much.” She smiled, soft now, genuine. “Thank you.”

Blue fox-fire bloomed in her palms. She moved through the warehouse methodically, burning every trace of Hiroto’s existence. Bones turned to ash, blood evaporated, cloth disintegrated. Even the smell disappeared, replaced by something clean and sharp like ozone. When she was done, the warehouse floor was spotless, as though nothing had ever happened here at all. Ryuji watched the string between them, bright, taut, perfect. Watched her turn to look at him, head tilted.

“What?”

“Did you make us hunt him down because he was fat?” He cocked a brow.

She blinked. Blinked again. Put on her most innocent expression. “Whaaat? Pfft, no. Never. I was helping you. He owed money!”

“Haeun.”

“…maybe a little?”

“You evil little…”

“You love me.” She grinned, holding up her pinkie, the red band gleaming in the dim light. “Universe says you have to.”

He crossed to her in three strides, hooked his pinkie with hers. “Unfortunately.”

“Unfortunately?” She pretended to pout.

“Best mistake fate ever made.” He leaned down and kissed her, tasting copper and something else underneath, something wild and inhuman that should have scared him and didn’t.

When they broke apart, she was smiling. “So. Yokohama ryokan for the anniversary?”

“Yeah.” He took her hand properly now, threading their fingers together as they walked toward the exit. “And we’re definitely skipping that Italian place.”

“What about the French place in Roppongi?”

“The one with the good wine list?”

“That’s the one!” She squeezed his hand. “Oh, and we should check out that café near my office. The one that allows pets on the patio.”

“Fuku would love that.” He thought of his brother’s cat, the pretentious white furball that only tolerated Ryuji because he gave the best chin scratches. “We’ll bring him next week. He’ll probably still judge me for it.”

“He judges everyone. It’s his whole personality.”

They emerged from the warehouse into the night air. The docks stretched out ahead, fairy lights still glowing between the lamp posts. The bad guitar player was gone, but a few couples remained, walking hand in hand along the water.

Ryuji and Haeun joined them, pinkies linked, red bands touching. To anyone watching, they were just another young couple enjoying the evening, maybe a little too well-dressed for the docks, but Tokyo was full of beautiful people doing beautiful things, and occasionally, terrible ones, in equally good shoes.

They walked in comfortable silence, close enough that their shoulders brushed with each step. The river lapped against the concrete. Somewhere across the water, a train horn sounded.

“287 years,” Haeun said suddenly, quietly.

He looked down at her. “Hm?”

She held up their joined hands, the red bands side by side. “I waited 287 years for this. For you.”

“Worth it?”

She looked up at him. Her smile was the real one: not the heiress mask, not the civilian act, not the predator’s grin. Just Haeun. Just his soulmate. “Every single day.”

He kissed the top of her head, and they kept walking. Behind them, the warehouse sat empty and clean. Ahead of them, the night stretched out full of possibilities: anniversary trips to plan, restaurants to try, a whole life to build together.
