The patrol above her house used to run on a rhythm, forty minutes, boots on gravel, predictable enough to plan around. Tonight the man on the roofline moved without one. Annoying. A pattern he could work with. A man pacing at random might look down at the wrong second.

He watched anyway. Waited.

Her house sat in a good part of town, at the front of the reinforced cluster. Better than the edges. The edges were still chewing people alive. Thunder rolled somewhere behind the clouds and the sky went that color again, the one it always went now, red and wrong, nothing like a sky should look.

Water pooled at his boots. He glanced at it once, then adjusted the mask, the long curved beak still carrying the faint reek of old marrow. The mask sat against his face the way it always did, bone fitted to bone, ground down from something that used to belong to an animal with a jaw of its own. Chipped along the left cheek from the fights out past the last checkpoint. He tipped it an inch to keep the rain out of the seam and went still again. The man on the roof drifted toward the far end of the yard.

That had been his window.

He crossed to the side door the way water finds a crack in concrete. He rattled the handle. Door’s locked. Smart, but useless. Lightning went white across the yard and he brought his boot down once. Wood splintered. The door swung in.

Tap. Tap. Tap.

Mud blooming dark on pale tile behind him. The television threw color across the room, a man and a woman laughing somewhere sun still fell on gardens, and she was curled beneath a knitted blanket in the middle of it, black hair slipping loose from its braid, one hand tucked under her cheek like a child’s.

Look at her.

Nasrin Yukiko Al-Hamid.

She hadn’t moved in three years. Not really. Even asleep she looked graceful, quiet warmth still clinging to her like it refused to leave. Still soft in all the places the world had gone sharp. Still asleep in front of a television while people four streets over were tearing each other apart for canned meat. He hated how peaceful she looked, like every sleepless violent night he had survived was a personal insult. She lived so easily. The world had ended and for her it was just another night with the rain and the glow of a screen. He had become this. Hulking. Unstable. Face that refused to stay still. And she remained unchanged.

Ya Rab. Why?

***Why why why?***

She wriggled under the blanket. Rain on the roof, voices on the screen, and still she slept like nothing could touch her. Same as always. The scrawny kid she never saw. Even now, with him this size, she did not notice.

Typical.

He wanted her to notice. Notice the danger. Notice him. The knife came free of its sheath with a soft scrape. He leaned over her. Her scent hit him first, something warm gone soft with sleep. His stomach twisted with loathing and his jaw locked forcing down anything else that did to him.

“Wake up.” The words scraped out of a ruined throat, rough and low.

She stirred. He watched, still as the mask on his face, waiting for the part where this went the way it was supposed to go. Her eyes opened slow. Unhurried. The way a person wakes up on an ordinary Tuesday. He tightened his grip on the knife, ready.

Instead her brows knit. She lifted one hand and touched the tip of the beak. “Birdy?”

Birdy?

He went rigid. Majnoona. Who did she think she was talking to?

“Don’t touch me.” The knife found her throat before he’d finished the sentence. She gasped, went still, and it wasn’t the stillness he’d spent three years imagining. She was looking at him. Actually looking, past the bone and the plastic, like she was waiting for him to get to the point. “Trying to play it cool?” It scraped out of him, half snarl. “You’ll die tonight.” He leaned in until the curve of the beak nearly brushed her nose, close enough that she’d have to breathe him in, iron and wet bone and something underneath that used to be human. “Do you think this is a game? Think the world’s still safe because your lights are on?”

“No.” She said it the way you’d correct someone about the weather. “It’s just, do I know you? You seem familiar.”

He stared at her from behind the mask. A blade sat at her pulse and she was asking about familiarity. “I have a knife to your throat,” he said, slow, like she might need it repeated, “and you’re wondering if we’ve met.”

“It’s your eyes.” Not raising her voice. Not doing anything a woman with a blade at her throat was supposed to do. “I remember someone with eyes like that. Take off your mask.”

Silence, filled up by rain on the roof and the tinny laugh track from the television. Then her breathing evened out again. That ticked him off. He stared down at her with everything predatory he had left, waiting for the high pitch, the scramble, the useless shove against a body twice her size. He wanted the tears. He wanted the terror that usually came with the sight of him. He wanted her to fight. He wanted her to finally see the monster.

“You want to see?” His voice cracked down the middle of the sentence. “You want to see what’s been watching you from the dark?”

“Yes.”

No hesitation. No flinch.

His fingers found the edges of the mask before he’d told them to. It came away with a crack of old plastic and the air of her living room hit his skin raw, too cool, too honest. He didn’t look away, and neither did she, taking in the matted hair, the hollow of his cheeks, the grey his skin had gone. He felt the shift start under the surface, the bones refusing to stay still, the face twitching with everything he could not lock down.

He let her see that too.

She blinked. Sat up. Nose to nose now, close enough that he flinched back before she did. Then she grinned like she’d found something she’d misplaced. “I knew it!” Warm. The voice you’d use on an old friend spotted across a crowded souk. “I’ve only ever seen eyes that pretty once. Azrael. How have you been?”

It was his turn to blink.

Ya Allah.

“I— what?” He found his voice about three seconds too late.

She kept going like she hadn’t heard him at all. “Wallah, you got so tall. Were you hitting the gym?”

“Gym.” His voice nearly cracked. “Wait. You said my eyes are pretty. These?” His eyes hadn’t been human in three years. Every scanner in Zahran flagged them on sight. She had looked straight into them and called them pretty.

“Yeah.” She nodded like this was obvious. “Always thought so. I liked sitting next to you in chem, I could look without it being weird, you know? Didn’t wanna full on stare.” She kept going, soft, unbothered, like she wasn’t handing him a live wire. “I used to hurry to our seat, scared someone else would talk it. I mean sitting next to you is better than walking up and going, ‘hey, chem partner, love your eyes’. Pretty sure you’d have told a teacher.” She glanced away, a little sheepish. “Yeah. Still sounds creepy out loud.”

The sound that came out of him wasn’t a laugh, close but not quite. He leaned in until his shadow swallowed her whole, until there was nowhere left for the television light to reach her face. “Creepy,” he said, the word vibrating against her mouth. “You think that’s creepy.”

“I mean, kind of—”

Boots hit the porch outside. She went rigid, her hand closing hard around his forearm, and for the first time since he’d broken her door down she looked like she was actually paying attention to the danger in the room. Voices, too many, too loud, moving up through the yard.

“Friends of yours?” she whispered.

“No.” The word came out serrated. “Not friends.”

He heard them clearer now, the stomp of boots that had never learned what quiet meant, voices wet with something they shouldn’t have been drinking this close to a Black Zone. He came down over her without deciding to, his weight settling across her like a second skin, some far-off part of him wondering exactly what he thought he was protecting her from when he was the one who’d broken the door in the first place.

“Hey, look at this place, plenty of shit to grab in here!” One of them found the door. “This door’s broken. Coming through!”

“Chalo, chalo,” another one laughed, further back.

Her eyes went wide. “You broke my back door?” A whisper yell, right against his jaw. “Not even the lock, the whole door?”

“It was in the way.”

He didn’t move off her. If anything he pressed down harder, a wall of weight meant to hide her, or maybe just meant to make sure nothing else in this house got to touch her first.

The air went out of her in a small, startled wheeze. “Ease up a little,” she managed, “and tell me how many.”

He tilted his head. “Three.” He murmured against her ear, “maybe four.”

He felt her shiver under him, gone quiet in a way that had nothing to do with fear. She didn’t look at him. She reached under the couch instead, and something clicked. A gun. Safety off before he even registered what she was holding, chambered a round, and pushed it into his hands.

“You caused this. Go fix it.”

He looked at the gun. Looked at her. “You have a gun.”

“Who doesn’t, habibi, it’s the apocalypse.” She pushed it further into his grip. “Now go fix the problem.”

“Fix it?” Low. Almost a threat.

“Fix it.” She didn’t blink.

“Khalas.” Fine.

He rolled off the couch in one motion, rising to his full height, mask sliding back into place as he crossed toward the sound of the dining room. The first man to round the corner was mid laugh, crowbar loose in one trembling hand, words dying somewhere in his throat as he looked up into a face that didn’t belong to a homeowner.

“Who the fuck are you?”

Aza didn’t answer. He raised the gun and settled the barrel against the man’s forehead. The man’s mouth was still open when the shot took him. The other three froze in the doorway. The laughing stopped. He didn’t wait for them to find their nerve. He could feel her behind him, a low, steady presence in the corner of the room, watching. That was enough.

The second one swung a bat wide and wild. Aza stepped outside the arc before it landed, drove the barrel into his gut, and fired twice. The third had a kitchen knife half drawn when the shot caught him in the throat, and the fourth didn’t get further than a step before it was over.

Silence rushed back into the room, thick with copper and gunpowder. He stood there a moment in the wreckage of her floor, breathing hard, something low and satisfied coiling in his chest that he didn’t examine too closely. Then he turned back toward the couch.

“It’s done.”

She peeked over the cushions, surveying the damage like she was checking on a garden after a storm. “Thank you.” A smile, easy, like he’d taken out the trash. “You hurt?”

“Hurt.” He crossed to her, blood drying dark on his knuckles, and cupped the side of her face without thinking about what he was smearing across her skin. “I’m fine.”

“Well, that’s good, because—”

She snatched the gun back out of his hand.

Bang!

Behind him, the one he’d gut shot stopped crawling. She’d taken the shot herself, clean, decisive, the recoil barely moving her small hands, gun still smoking in her grip. A grin spread across his face, all teeth, no warmth in it and all the wrong kind of pride. She wasn’t soft. Or she was soft and this too, both at once, a pastel colored thing that spilled blood as easily as it poured tea. His blood did something ugly and pleased at the same time. He reached out and wrapped his hand around the barrel, lowering it for her. He didn’t pull her away from the mess on her floor. He pulled her into him instead.

Nasrin squeaked, pink flooding her cheeks. “Hey— what are you doing?” She hit the cushions with a startled squeak, pinned there under the full weight of him, no hesitation in it this time. She gasped, then laughed. “Hey, wait! Azrael—”

“You shoot a man,” he said against her throat, “and then tell me to wait. You’re out of your mind.”

Her breath caught. Her whole body went taut, then loose under him and she laughed. “Habibi, wait—”

“Wait for what?”

“Oh, come on.” She was still laughing, pink to the tips of her ears. “We can’t do this here, I have a door to fix and bodies to sort and there’s actually a lot to clean up—”

“Cleaning? You want to clean while the world is ending.” Her hand found his at her waistband and she went pinker.

“Seriously, up, we have work to do. I can’t let good meat go to waste, rigor’s gonna set in if we wait too long.”

He froze.

“Meat?” He sat up slowly and looked left, then right, then over his shoulder at the four bodies cooling on her floor. “You’re not talking about—”

“Do you see chickens in here? Or goats?” She was already pushing up off the couch, rolling her sleeves like this was chore day. “Up. Help me drag them to the basement.”

He got up on autopilot, watching her step over a dead man like he was a rug that needed straightening.
