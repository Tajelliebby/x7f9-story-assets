Adelaide

Adelaide rose before dawn to work alongside her Spring faction at the shelters. Her golden hair tied back simply, she ladled porridge from deep pots while bread was distributed to the destitute. The work was hot and endless: stirring, serving, rotating with other stewards until every soul was fed.

After that came the rush to compose herself, don her ceremonial robes, and reach the Summit of Seasons for the morning devotions. Today she would lead the morning devotions in place of the noon ones, having swapped with Rowena, who had matters to investigate since the Aedra breach in the capital. Adelaide raised her hands and voice before the gathered crowd, offering supplication to the Light on their behalf and thanking Iltheris for His continued mercy. But she felt little movement in the air around her, and her heart ached. Yes, He would not come where He was not welcome. The Light was not so forceful, though sometimes she wished He were. She opened her eyes and saw the worldly cares etched on rich and poor faces alike. Many seemed to come seeking spectacle rather than genuine worship.

Indeed, it is a wicked generation that asks for a sign.

She swallowed, trying not to feel disappointed as she handed the ceremony over to the priest who would deliver the morning sermon. Her eyes caught another’s across the crowd, and her heart stuttered. The northern duke? How curious.

After the procession ended, she jogged over to him. “Your Grace, good morning!” He greeted her with polite formality, and she bit back the urge to tease him about his rigid propriety. Not this time, at least. “What brings you out so early?” she asked instead.

“Curiosity,” he answered honestly. “It surprises me that the people of the capital rise so early for worship. I thought them all… easy-going.”

How peculiar that someone from the frozen north seemed more interested in knowing the Light than those of the capital who unknowingly saw His works every day. The thought made her giggle. She put her hands on her hips. “Is your curiosity satisfied?”

“Somewhat,” he hummed. “And what are your plans for the day?”

When she listed her activities, helping at the shelter and infirmary, and invited him to join her, he hesitated before agreeing. That was all Adelaide needed to hear. Rurik returned to the shelter around one o’clock, after the noon toll. He arrived on horseback, dressed much more simply than before, though Adelaide noticed he still wore long sleeves and gloves despite the warmth.

“I thought you might not come,” she joked.

“I was looking for appropriate clothes,” he replied honestly.

Adelaide laughed. “We’re not serving nobles here. No one cares about such things.” She paused, studying him. “Do you know how to cook?”

“Only around a campfire on the battlefield.”

“Perfect. You’ll be serving, then.”

Before he could protest, she had produced a pink apron and was tying it around his waist. He felt heat rise in his cheeks for reasons he couldn’t name. As they cleaned and prepared the dining area, wiping tables, setting out plates and utensils, sweeping and mopping, Rurik admitted, “I did not know the Archons do these things.”

“We give our service freely, yes, though I more so than the rest.”

“Why?”

“Because I like it. And because it’s my role as the Spring Archon.” She paused in her work, turning to bump his shoulder playfully. He stiffened and she quickly apologized. “Each season has roles, and roles within those roles. Mine involves the most contact with common folk.” Her expression grew more serious. “Healing, restoration, mercy. We’re meant to be healers, caretakers, pacifists… though we no longer abide by pacifism since the first Rift opened. The healers had to take up bow and sword.” She looked at her own hands. “Did you know that in the Verdant Choir, from the humblest bonne to myself, our doctrine is ‘Blood shall not stain hands made to mend’? Yet so many of us have had to fight.” Adelaide’s smile turned rueful. “While I’m talented with a bow, I also learned swordsmanship. I’m quite skilled, if I do say so myself. I have never taken a life myself, thank the Light.”

Rurik nodded, realizing this was what she’d meant about being a knight. He’d known all Archons went to the front lines, but hadn’t thought they would actually be fighting. “Of course, My Lady.” Adelaide pouted at his formal tone, and Rurik found himself holding back something that felt suspiciously like laughter. “And the others?” he asked quickly.

“Sister Rowena heads the Stewards of the Bough, the knights you see mixed with the guards patrolling the city. Internal peacekeeping, rural patrols, domestic order. Brother Kaelen is Marshal of the Blazing Host, our main army, always on the front lines while the Verdant Choir and I stay in the rearguard… usually.” Her expression darkened. “And Brother Thorne has the most challenging role.”

“Challenging?”

“The Silent Veil, the Winter Archon’s inquisition. They bear the weight of decisions I’m grateful I’ve never had to make.” Her voice grew somber. “To have to take a life, especially to defend your own… I can’t imagine what he says to the Light when he prays.”

Rurik looked at his own hands that had shed blood, then curled them into fists. “It is necessary.”

“Sadly so,” she agreed.

The conversation turned to how the priests of the Summit had become knights when the Rift opened. Legends told of a time when the world was whole, untouched by corruption, but the Therii grew arrogant, questioning Iltheris and seeking power beyond what was meant for them. Then the earth split open, and the Aedra poured out on the earth.

“Is that why the Archons no longer stay at the Summit?” Rurik asked. “Because you actively participate in war?” Adelaide’s expression grew confused, so he clarified. “Because you shed blood.”

“Oh, yes. There are requirements for living at the Summit of Seasons, which is why some Archons over the years never lived there during their entire tenure. Murder is one of the prohibitions, so we lost that right when the rifts opened.”

“Even though the Aedra are demonic creatures?”

“You know quite well we fight not only Aedra but also those who worship them.” Rurik nodded grimly. He had lost his father to one of the Betrayers, humans who had embraced Aedra power, believing them to be the true rulers of the world. “Many of them were once from the Order,” Adelaide continued, “having succumbed to forbidden knowledge. To have to defend yourself from someone who was once your ally…”

“But you told me yourself you’ve never taken a life.”

“True, but on the battlefield I’ve thought about it, imagined it. I think I’m already guilty of shedding blood in my heart. Regardless, I want to be where my brothers and sisters are. Why should I be the only one at the Summit?”

Rurik felt perplexed by her reasoning but left it alone as people came rushing in for the meal service. This was a different kind of fast-paced battlefield, and he felt his chest tighten as women eyed him the way they eyed the stew.

He swallowed his pride. “My Lady, might we swap positions?”

Her brows knitted in confusion, but then her expression relaxed. “Of course.”

In the kitchen, the staff immediately reminded him of his station by tiptoeing around him. Adelaide had made him feel so normal he’d momentarily forgotten his rank, though he couldn’t imagine being too intimidating while wearing a pink apron. He followed instructions well, ladling portions of stew and rice, occasionally glancing out at Adelaide as she interacted with the crowd. She seemed to… glow slightly?

“Does Archon Spring always shine like that?” he asked one of the kitchen staff.

“Oh yes, she usually glows a bit after the devotions.”

Rurik’s brows nearly vanished into his hairline. What part of someone glowing was normal? Do they all glow?

After lunch service ended, Adelaide collected dishes while Rurik washed. Outside, she rolled her shoulders with a sigh and stretched her arms overhead. His lips almost quirked upward before he forced the expression back down.

“Please don’t stretch like that. We’re outside,” he gently chided.

“But there’s no one here?”

“Still…” Why did he bother?

“Ah!” She turned to him suddenly. “Have you tried our sorbets?”

“Your what?”

Adelaide gasped as if he had blasphemed. “You have to try one before you leave the capital.” She took him by the cuff of his sleeve and tugged him along.

Rurik looked down at where her thumb and forefinger pinched the fabric. He needed to arrange for a chaperone when going out with Adelaide, but he didn’t know any married women in the capital. His temple was already beginning to throb. The sorbet shop bustled with activity, though the simply dressed patrons didn’t seem cause for concern. Adelaide entered first, greeted warmly by the proprietors. She rummaged through her tunic pockets and produced a coin purse, placing six pence on the counter.

“I’d like two small sorbets today, please.”

“Small ones?” the older woman asked, while the younger stared at Rurik. He pretended to look out the window.

“Yes! This gentleman has never tried one, so I wanted two samples.”

“Is that so?” The woman beamed. “Welcome, sir. We have several flavours: lemon, orange, strawberry, raspberry, apricot…”

Rurik’s expression began to crinkle as she listed the fruits. Adelaide interrupted. “You don’t like sweets, do you?”

“Not particularly,” he murmured.

“Hmm.” She gave it some thought. “Aunty, do you have any that aren’t sweet?”

“Yes, we have tea and vegetable options.”

Rurik’s expression relaxed. Adelaide grinned. “A black tea and cucumber ice, please.”

“Right away.”

She led Rurik to sit by the window, where he could easily check his surroundings. “I’m sorry,” Adelaide suddenly said.

“For?” He looked confused.

“I should have taken you to the centre district instead, treated you to cool cream. I hear the coffee is popular among gentlemen. Brother Kaelen quite liked it.”

“Oh no, this is perfectly fine,” he quickly replied, forcing a smile. Anything but that. He preferred to avoid nobles when possible, especially alone with Adelaide. If her reputation were smeared because of him…

He took a moment to really look at the simply dressed woman across from him. Does she have a reputation to ruin?

“What?” she asked, noticing his scrutiny.

“Nothing…” Rurik slowly narrowed his eyes, and Adelaide giggled.

Yes, I doubt there’s any reputation to ruin.

“Your ices,” a young voice interrupted. The server had brought a tray with beautifully plated sorbets in what looked like glass cups.

The girl inched closer, and Adelaide noticed how Rurik subtly pressed himself against the window. Standing quickly, Adelaide took the tray herself. “Thank you,” she beamed, and the girl hesitated before nodding and leaving them alone.

Adelaide settled back into her seat, lifting her eyes to regard the duke. It was the second time today she’d seen him retreat when a woman approached. She wanted to ask, but sorbets were more important right now.

“Try it,” she beamed at him.

Rurik nodded and pulled the black tea sorbet closer. It looked like softened ice, already melting slightly with garnishes on top. He took a spoonful. Earthy, slightly bitter, refreshing. It melted on his tongue and cooled him from within, so he took another bite.

Looking up, he found Adelaide resting her elbow on the table, cheeks pressed against her palms, eyes crinkled in a smile as she watched him. “Do you like it?”

“Elbows off the table,” he chided. “Yes, it’s quite refreshing.”

“Let me try.” Before he could object, she had scooped some with her own spoon. “Hmm…” She swallowed thoughtfully. “I think I prefer my fruit sorbets.” The comment drew a brief sound from him, and she lit up. “You laughed!”

“I did not. I was simply in awe of you.”

She huffed playfully. “Do you want to try the cucumber?”

He considered, then nodded. She quickly scooped some, but he rested a gloved finger on her wrist before she could lift the spoon. “Lady Adelaide, please have some decorum.”

“What did I do this time?”

It’s not what you did. It’s what you were about to do. Instead, he smiled at her. “Enjoy that one. I’ll buy one for myself before we depart.”

She frowned. “What if you don’t like it? Just taste this one.”

“If I don’t like it, you can have it,” he bargained.

She considered this, then nodded and returned to her own cup. He sighed with relief. A disaster narrowly spared.
