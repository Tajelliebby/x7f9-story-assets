The walk back to the shelter proved pleasant enough, with Adelaide chattering about the quality of the sorbets and lamenting the flavours she hadn’t tried. Rurik found himself oddly focused on the musical quality of her voice, impossibly bright, utterly audacious, as she gestured with her hands while speaking.

“You never bought the one you promised yourself,” she said suddenly, turning to face him with those vivid green eyes wide with realization. “Oh, we should have, but it’s too late to turn back now, isn’t it?”

“Indeed,” he replied, watching her face fall with what appeared to be genuine distress over his missed dessert.

At the shelter, he collected his destrier while Adelaide waited, absently running her fingers along the weathered wooden fence. Then it was a short walk to the Order’s headquarters.

“Thank you for walking me back,” she beamed before trotting towards the entrance. Adelaide moved like a whirlwind, a storm of spring that had crashed into his life like a reckless gale. He had pictured a Summoner of Spring as someone soft and delicate, all flowing robes and gentle smiles.

Adelaide proved him wrong daily.

Rurik watched her disappear inside the main building before mounting his steed. The ride to the Imperial Palace gave him too much time to think. About her. About them. He’d barely dismounted when a frantic servant rushed toward him.

“Your Grace! Your Grace, thank the Light you’ve returned!”

“What has occurred?” Rurik handed his reins to a stable boy.

“Your messenger, my lord. Garen has returned, but he’s injured. Captain Harald is with him in the east wing.”

Rurik’s blood chilled. He strode through the palace corridors with purpose, his boots echoing against marble floors. In the east wing, he found Harald’s imposing figure hunched over a cot where Garen lay, his face pale and bandaged.

“Report,” Rurik commanded quietly.

Harald straightened, his cold grey eyes meeting his lord’s without flinching. “Aedra, my lord. They struck his party half a day north of the capital. Garen barely escaped.”

“The message?”

Garen struggled to sit up, wincing. “Forgive me, Your Grace. I couldn’t… they came from nowhere. The letter may yet be in my saddlebags, though I never delivered it personally.”

Rurik placed a steadying hand on the young man’s shoulder. “Rest. You’ve done well to return alive.” But privately, ice settled in his veins. Aedra activity this far inland, breaching the Borderwall. This was no random incursion. This was organized.

“The reports weren’t exaggerated,” Harald said grimly once they’d stepped into the corridor. “They’re moving inland.”

“Prepare the men. I’ll need to speak with the Emperor immediately.”

“How many shall I ready for departure?”

“A small company. We leave within two days, with or without…” He stopped himself. Without what? Without whom?

Harald’s sharp gaze didn’t miss the hesitation. “My lord?”

“Just prepare them.” An hour later, Rurik found himself in the Emperor’s private study, where Ardentis looked up from his correspondence, grinning. Rurik paused, eyes narrowing. “Cousin.”

“Rurik! How delightful. How was your afternoon with Lady Adelaide?”

“I haven’t time for pleasantries. I need to return to Aurenfell immediately.”

Ardentis’s expression shifted, the casual smile fading as he set down his quill. “What has happened?” Rurik relayed Garen’s report efficiently, watching his cousin’s face grow grave. “Aedra breaching the Borderwall,” Ardentis murmured. “This changes things considerably.”

“Indeed. Which is why I must depart within-”

“Take Adelaide with you.”

“I beg your pardon?”

“You heard me quite clearly.” Ardentis leaned back in his chair, fingers steepled. “A Spring Summoner’s power could prove invaluable against Aedra corruption, and frankly, the woman needs to see more of the empire she’ll help govern.”

“Absolutely not. The journey alone presents dangers, not to mention…”

“She’s an Archon, Rurik. She’s faced far worse than a carriage ride north.”

“The political implications…”

“Are precisely why this makes perfect sense. You’re engaged. It’s natural she’d want to see your holdings, meet your people.” Ardentis’s smile returned, sharp and knowing. “Unless you object to her company for other reasons?”

Rurik felt heat creep up his neck. “That’s hardly the point.”

“Then we’re settled. Though I do suggest getting her opinion on the matter before making the decision for her. Adelaide strikes me as someone who prefers to be consulted.”

Two hours later, Rurik sat at his desk, attempting to craft a letter that didn’t sound either like a military dispatch or an invitation to a morning call. He’d written three versions and burned them all, finally settling on his fifth attempt explaining the situation and the Emperor’s suggestion. He gave himself two days to prepare. He would leave with or without her when the time came.

In hindsight, he should’ve expected the commotion at his door the following morning.

“Your Grace?” his appointed butler called through the door. “Her Verdancy, Keeper of Blooming Life, requests an audience.”

Rurik blinked. “She’s here? Now?”

“Indeed, my lord. Shall I…”

The door opened without ceremony, and Adelaide swept in, her eyes bright with excitement. “Good morning, Your Grace. I received your letter and wanted to discuss…” She stopped, apparently noticing his stunned expression. “Is something amiss?”

“You came to my chambers. Alone.”

“Oh.” She glanced around. “Should I not have? I was rather eager to give you my answer.”

“Perhaps we should adjourn to the Emperor’s study,” he managed, rising from his desk.

“If you prefer, though I fail to see why it matters given we’re betrothed.” She tilted her head with that particular expression he was learning to recognize, the one that meant she was about to say something that would leave him speechless. “But very well. Lead the way.”

In the Emperor’s study, with Ardentis beaming like a cat who’d discovered cream, Adelaide clasped her hands together.

“I would be absolutely delighted to accompany you north, Your Grace. I’ve never seen Aurenfell, and the opportunity to witness your homeland sounds marvellous.”

“The journey presents considerable risks,” Rurik felt compelled to point out.

“I am an Archon,” she replied simply. “Risk and I are well acquainted.”

More like she’s acquainted with recklessness. Rurik already felt tired.

“Then it’s settled,” Ardentis declared. “Should anyone inquire, Her Verdancy wishes to see the north before your wedding. Perfectly reasonable.”

It wasn’t a lie, which seemed to satisfy Adelaide completely. “Excellent,” Adelaide continued brightly. “Though I confess I need your assistance, Your Grace. I’ve never travelled to the northern regions and haven’t the faintest idea what to bring or purchase for the journey.”

“Have you informed your companions at the Order of your departure from the capital?” Rurik asked, still processing the speed at which events were unfolding.

“Naturally.”

Once again, he found himself surprised by her audacious directness. “Of course you have.”

“I’ve been saving my allowance for some time now, so the expense should prove no difficulty.”

Ardentis cleared his throat. “My dear Lady Adelaide, I would be honoured to cover any expenses…”

“How kind, but unnecessary. I shall purchase my own provisions.”

The Emperor’s expression resembled a parent whose child had just refused an offered sweet. Rurik bit back what might have been a laugh. “If we’re quite finished,” Ardentis said with wounded dignity, “perhaps you might see to those preparations.”

“Wait,” Rurik added quickly, “we’ll require a married woman to accompany us as chaperone.”

Adelaide brightened. “Oh, Elaine would be perfect! She’s married, and everyone knows she was my nanny before I came of age. She’s been with me since I was nine.”

Another wave of inexplicable stress washed over him. “Very well. We depart tomorrow at dawn.”

“Wonderful! Shall we go shopping now?”

“I will follow the lady.”

They took a carriage to the Order’s headquarters. Rurik had kept the windows shuttered against prying eyes, though he didn’t mention it was for Adelaide’s sake when she questioned the darkness. Once they arrived, Adelaide slipped out of the carriage, and when she emerged from the building she had changed from her simple tunic and brought with her a woman whose sharp blue eyes reminded him forcibly of winter mornings. Elaine, for this could only be the famous nanny, took one look at Adelaide climbing into the carriage and immediately pinched her charge’s arm.

“Adelaide Veridis, where are your manners? Sit properly.”

“Ow! Elaine, that hurt.”

“Good. Perhaps it will remind you to behave like the lady you’re supposed to be.” Elaine turned to Rurik with a crisp curtsy. “Your Grace, please forgive my charge’s complete abandonment of etiquette. I fear responsibility for her deportment rests squarely with me.”

“The fault is hardly yours, madam,” Rurik replied as the coachman helped her into the carriage, finding himself oddly comforted by the woman’s brisk, unyielding manner.

Once they were underway, Adelaide attempted to loop her arm with the woman beside her. “Elaine has been with me since I was nine years old.”

“While that is true, please sit like a lady,” Elaine scolded, firmly disentangling herself.

“But no one can see us,” Adelaide protested, her lower lip jutting out.

“His Grace can see you.”

“He’s going to be my husband, so it hardly matters.”

Elaine pressed her fingers to her temple. “That’s all the more reason to behave properly.”

Adelaide’s giggle filled the carriage, and Rurik found himself studying her animated face. Something had been troubling him for some days, and he couldn’t let it rest. “Lady Adelaide,” he said carefully, “might I inquire about your thoughts regarding annulment?”

Her brow furrowed. “I’m afraid I don’t follow.”

He leaned back, thoughts swirling. What could she gain from marrying him? He was a duke, yes, but no more than a provincial lord compared to her position. Why would she, with her immense influence, consider such a match?

Perhaps it was simply convenience, a temporary solution she could discard at will. Unlike a lady of the court, she possessed the freedom to pursue an annulment whenever it suited her. He gazed at his gloved hands, picturing her slipping from his life as effortlessly as she’d entered it.

“Your Grace?” Her voice called him back to the present.

He met her eyes, expecting to find calculation or offence. Instead, Adelaide looked merely curious, without the sly manoeuvring he’d grown to expect from courtiers. A small mercy of her nature was this directness, and he found himself grateful for it.

“I simply wondered if you might… reconsider this arrangement at some future date.”

Adelaide’s expression softened with what looked remarkably like understanding. “Your Grace, I am and shall remain your wife. Archons don’t marry often, but when we do, it’s for life. Please don’t worry, I’ve already accepted any responsibility that comes with marrying you.”

Again, he couldn’t understand her. Why did it seem as though she was comforting him? Still, it didn’t answer his fundamental question.

Their shopping expedition proved more challenging than expected. Heavy winter clothing was hardly common in the capital’s temperate climate, but they managed to find adequate provisions for the journey north. “I shall purchase proper northern clothing for you once we arrive in Aurenfell,” he promised as they examined a rather thin cloak that would have to suffice.

Adelaide’s eyes lit up with genuine excitement. “Northern clothes? How wonderful! Do you think they’ll look well on me?”

He tilted his head, trying to imagine her slight frame swathed in the heavy furs and thick wools of his people. The image made him sputter unexpectedly.

Adelaide froze, her face falling. “Would I look that dreadful?”

“Forgive me,” he said quickly. “More than anything, you’d look… small.”

She blinked up at him with a confused smile that made his lips fight not to twitch upward. Indeed, she was already small and rather…

What am I thinking?

“We’re finished shopping. You need to pack.”

“But I haven’t seen half the…”

“You need to pack,” he repeated firmly, steering her toward the carriage.

An hour later, after delivering Adelaide safely to the Order, Rurik found himself questioning the wisdom of every decision that had led him to this moment. Specifically, he was questioning how he’d landed himself in the drawing room of the Imperial Palace annex with Duke Lawson and Viscount Eve.

His cousin, it seemed, had already loosed his tongue.

“Engaged!” Duke Lawson boomed, his sun-reddened face radiating betrayal. “And you didn’t see fit to inform me personally? Rurik, lad, I’ve known you since you were in leading strings!”

This is exactly why he didn’t want Lawson to know. The whole empire would know before he even left the capital.

“My dear Lawson,” Viscount Eve interjected smoothly, “perhaps we might offer our congratulations rather than our complaints?” Lawson’s indignation deflated slightly. “You have our blessing, Rurik. And our hopes for a safe journey north.”

Rurik accepted their well-wishes and showed them out before Lawson could launch into another tirade. He rested his head against the door with a sigh. How much more would Adelaide disrupt his peaceful life?
