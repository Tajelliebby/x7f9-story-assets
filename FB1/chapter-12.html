Early morning mist clung to the cobblestones outside the imperial annex as the four Archons gathered around Rurik’s carriage. Their voices carried on the cool air, bright with farewell wishes and promises to write. Rurik watched from beside his horse, studying Adelaide’s animated gestures as she embraced each of her fellow Archons in turn.

Would she still smile so brightly when faced with the realities of life on the frontier? The thought of her small frame bundled in heavy furs made something warm unfurl in his chest, quickly followed by concern. She was accustomed to comfort, to warmth, to the refined luxuries of court life. What had he agreed to in bringing her north?

“The horses are ready, Your Grace,” Harald interrupted his brooding. “Fresh mounts at every way station, as you requested.”

Rurik looked up from adjusting his stirrups. “And the escort?”

“Twenty men. Armed and provisioned for a fortnight’s travel.”

Rurik glanced at his soldiers standing at ready attention, then nodded. It would have to be sufficient, though they would need to replenish at villages along the way for the potentially eighteen-day journey. He couldn’t shorten it any more than that with company, but for Adelaide’s sake, he wanted to reach the mountains as quickly as possible. Perhaps he should take Viscount Eve up on that suggestion of a proper road after all.

“We leave within the hour.” He turned to Adelaide. “You and your chaperone will travel in the carriage, My Lady. I’ll ride alongside.”

“Splendid!” she chirped, bouncing slightly on her toes.

Rurik paused, studying her eager expression. “Are you quite certain about this, My Lady?”

“Absolutely!”

He wondered if she truly understood what she was agreeing to, not just the journey, but everything that followed. Marriage to him would mean isolation from everything she knew, responsibility for people who might never fully accept an outsider, and the constant threat of danger from the borderlands. Yet she had agreed without hesitation.

“Very well. Say your farewells.”

Adelaide helped Elaine into the carriage, and Maris and Cecily into a second, smaller one behind it, where the trunks and the rest of her retinue would ride. Only Elaine would share her carriage. Then she returned to where the Archons had formed a circle, joining hands with bowed heads. Rurik looked away, busying himself with final preparations, but he could hear the soft murmur of their benediction carried on the morning breeze. When he glanced back, Thorne was approaching. The Winter Archon’s ice-blue eyes held something that made Rurik straighten.

“May the Light guide your path and guard your steps,” Thorne said, his voice low and formal. “May His mercy shelter you from the storms ahead, and His strength sustain you when darkness falls.”

Rurik understood then that their journey north would not be entirely smooth.

Adelaide waved from the carriage window as they rolled through Valestra’s gates, her ribbon-tied hair catching the early sunlight. The city sprawled around them, grey stone buildings with smoke curling from countless chimneys, elegant shopfronts displaying their wares, and cobblestone streets coming alive with morning trade. The streets were rich with fresh bread from bakeries, exotic spices from merchants’ stalls, and the earthy scent of horses and leather.

Half a day beyond the gates, Harald, who had ridden ahead at dawn, met them at the stretch of road where Garen had been ambushed. He reported scorched verge and trampled earth and nothing more; whatever had struck the messenger was long gone. Rurik rode past it in silence, one hand resting on his sword hilt, and noticed Adelaide’s gaze linger on the blackened grass from the carriage window, her lips moving in a brief prayer. Neither of them spoke of it.

The first days passed swiftly on well-maintained turnpike roads. Their horses moved at a steady pace, changed regularly at coaching inns where ostlers rushed to attend ducal livery and innkeepers bowed low with practised deference. The countryside rolled past in gentle waves: manicured fields where sheep grazed in contentment, prosperous farms with their lambing pens busy, and tidy villages with temple spires. Adelaide pressed her face to the carriage window frequently, exclaiming over everything from the architectural details of manor houses to the way morning mist clung to meadow grass. Her enthusiasm proved infectious, drawing even reserved Elaine into animated conversations about the changing landscape.

At their first stop, Rurik approached the carriage with measured steps. “How do you fare, My Lady? The roads are smoother here, but I’m told they grow more challenging ahead.”

“Wonderfully well,” Adelaide replied, her cheeks pink with excitement. “Everything is so beautifully varied. In the capital, one grows accustomed to sameness, but out here each mile brings something new to discover.”

“You find novelty appealing?”

“I find life appealing,” she corrected with a smile. “And life, I’m learning, exists most fully in places I’ve never been.”

By the third day, subtle changes began to emerge. Other carriages on the road grew fewer, replaced by farm carts and the occasional merchant’s wagon. The inns, while still respectable, lacked the refined elegance of those near the capital. Adelaide noticed too, commenting on the heartier fare and the more direct manner of the inn staff.

“They’re friendlier here,” she observed over a simple but filling supper of mutton stew and brown bread. “Less… careful with their words.”

“They have less reason for caution,” Rurik replied. “Political intrigue holds little interest when your concerns are harvest and weather.”

“How refreshing that must be.”

Again, her perspective surprised him. Most courtiers would have found such simplicity beneath their notice.

On the move once again, the air began to carry a sharper bite as they travelled further north. Rolling hills gave way to more dramatic landscapes, and Adelaide’s breath started to mist when she spoke during their morning departures. She had taken to wrapping herself in an additional shawl, though she never complained about the cold.

On the sixth day, they encountered their first real industry, a cluster of buildings with smoking chimneys that darkened the sky for miles around. The contrast to the pastoral countryside was stark, and Adelaide stared in fascination at the workers streaming from the mills at day’s end.

“What do they make there?” she asked, watching the steady flow of people.

“Textiles, mostly. Wool from the northern flocks, processed and woven,” Rurik explained, following her gaze.

“It seems so… contained. All that work happening within those walls.” She studied the smoking chimneys thoughtfully. “Yet necessary, I suppose.”

“Efficient, though less pleasant than the old methods.”

“Progress often comes at a cost, doesn’t it?”

He blinked, but chose silence. That evening found them at a coaching inn larger than most they’d encountered, its common room buzzing with conversation from travellers heading in all directions. Adelaide had requested they take their meal downstairs rather than in their private parlour, curious about the mixing of different social stations.

“I’ve rarely had opportunity to observe such variety,” she explained when Rurik raised an eyebrow at her request. “Merchants, farmers, craftsmen, all sharing the same space and conversation.”

They found a corner table where Adelaide could watch the room’s dynamics while they ate. A group of wool traders discussed prices with animated gestures, while a travelling tinker regaled other patrons with news from distant villages. The atmosphere was convivial, honest, and entirely unlike the carefully orchestrated interactions of court life.

“Tell me,” Adelaide said, sampling the inn’s excellent beef pie, “have you travelled much beyond Aurenfell? Before your duties called you south, I mean.”

“Some. I’ve been to Risenholt on diplomatic matters, and various border territories for military purposes. But my knowledge of the empire’s interior remains limited.”

Adelaide’s face lit up. “You’ve been to Risenholt? Then you’ve seen where Kaelen trained! But not Sundermarsh?”

“Not Sundermarsh, no.”

“Oh, you would love it,” she said, leaning forward with enthusiasm. “It’s nothing like the capital at all. Sundermarsh sprawls along the coast. The harbour is always alive with ships from everywhere, and you can hear a dozen languages in a single morning at the markets.”

Her accent began to shift as she spoke, the careful court pronunciation giving way to something warmer, more musical. The change reminded Rurik suddenly of Duke Lawson and his animated storytelling.

“The sun there is nothing like the gentle light we have here,” Adelaide continued, her gestures becoming more expressive. “It beats down with such force that everyone develops this lovely bronze colour, much darker than I am now. I used to spend hours in my mother’s herb garden, and by summer’s end I looked like I’d been crafted from amber.”

“Your mother was an herb trader?”

“The most successful in the Lower Market district,” Adelaide said proudly. “She taught me everything about reading soil, understanding seasons, predicting which plants would thrive where. Our stall was famous for having the freshest imports from trading ships, spices that hadn’t even reached the Upper Market yet. Oh, and the food! Fish stews with spices from the eastern kingdoms, bread flavoured with herbs that grow only in southern climates, fruits preserved in methods learned from northern traders.”

“It sounds vibrant,” Rurik said, watching her animated expression with growing fascination.

“Chaotic and wonderful and never, ever quiet,” Adelaide agreed with a laugh.

Before Rurik could respond, Cecily appeared at Adelaide’s elbow with gentle insistence. “My Lady, perhaps it’s time to retire? Tomorrow’s journey begins early.”

Adelaide glanced at the darkened windows with surprise. “Goodness, how long have we been talking?”

Cecily chuckled. “Your Grace, thank you for indulging her stories. She rarely has opportunity to speak of home.”

As Adelaide rose to leave, she paused beside Rurik’s chair. “Thank you for listening. I hope I haven’t bored you with tales of market stalls and harbour life.”

“Not at all,” he replied honestly. “Your descriptions make me curious to see this Sundermarsh for myself.”

“Perhaps someday you shall,” she said with a smile that lingered as she followed her maids upstairs.

Rurik remained at the table longer than necessary; there was much to contemplate.

The next morning brought their first real taste of northern weather. Frost glittered on the grass, and their breath misted in the sharp air. Adelaide emerged from the inn bundled in her warmest cloak, cheeks pink with cold but spirits undaunted.

“It’s quite invigorating,” she declared, though her voice carried a slight tremor from the chill.

“This is nothing,” Rurik warned as he helped her into the carriage. “Wait until we reach the hills proper.”

The landscape around them had begun its true transformation. Trees appeared more gnarled, fields more sparse, and the overall impression was one of greater exposure to the elements. Villages became fewer and farther between, their inhabitants hardier-looking folk who watched their passage with curious but guarded eyes. As they climbed into more elevated country, the air grew thin enough to make Adelaide’s breathing visible in steady puffs. She continued pointing out sights that caught her interest: a hawk circling overhead, ice forming along stream banks, the way snow clung to bare tree branches like nature’s own decoration.

But Rurik found himself watching her more than the landscape, noting how she rubbed her hands together between observations, how she pulled her cloak tighter with each mile north. The concern that had been nagging at him since Valestra grew stronger. On the tenth day, they could no longer find suitable inns for the night. The last village had been too small to offer proper accommodation for their party, and darkness was falling fast over increasingly wild terrain.

“We’ll make camp,” Rurik decided, surveying a clearing beside a stream that looked defensible. “Set up the travelling pavilions.”

Adelaide emerged from the carriage before he could assist her, surveying their temporary camp with interest rather than dismay. “How lovely. I haven’t slept under stars since my last campaign season.” The soldiers looked uncertain until she began efficiently organizing their cooking supplies.

“My Lady…”

“I’ve done this before,” she assured Harald when he attempted to protest. “At the shelters, and during campaigns. Many hands make light work.”

She proved her words, working alongside the men to prepare their evening meal, adding herbs from her own stores that transformed simple camp fare into something approaching a feast. After the meal, while most of the party settled around the main campfire, Adelaide disappeared into the gathering dusk. Rurik helped serve the last portions of stew, then realized he hadn’t seen her for several minutes.

“Where…” he began, then felt a moment of genuine panic at her absence.

“She went that way,” Elaine said quietly, pointing toward a grove of trees beyond the firelight. “Taking her evening prayers, I’d expect.”

Rurik found Adelaide kneeling in a small clearing, her hands clasped tightly before her, lips moving quickly. Sweat beaded on her brow despite the cool evening air. He hesitated, uncertain whether to interrupt, then settled quietly beside her with two bowls of stew.

She seemed unaware of his presence, her focus completely absorbed in whatever communion she sought. Rurik watched her posture change, kneeling, then crouching, then kneeling again, and wondered what could require such earnest supplication. He used his own bowl to cover hers, keeping her meal warm as minutes passed.

A particularly cold gust swept through the clearing, making Adelaide shiver despite her concentration. Without thinking, Rurik shrugged off his coat and draped it around her shoulders. The gesture made her eyes open, startled back to awareness of her surroundings.

“Oh!” she breathed, blinking as though surfacing from deep water. “I didn’t realize… how long have I been…”

“Long enough for your supper to need warming,” Rurik said gently, offering her the bowl. “The stars are particularly bright tonight.”

Adelaide accepted the food gratefully, following his gaze upward. Away from any city’s glow, the night sky blazed with countless points of light.

“They seem so much closer here,” she observed between spoonfuls of stew. “In the capital, stars feel decorative. Here, they feel like… companions.”

“You find comfort in that?”

“I find wonder in it.” She pulled his coat closer around her shoulders. “Do you think the same stars shine over Aurenfell?”

“The same stars shine everywhere, My Lady.”

“But do they look the same? Feel the same?”

Rurik considered her question seriously. “The northern sky is clearer. Harder. Beautiful in its own way, but unforgiving.”

“Like the land itself?”

“Yes.”

Adelaide nodded thoughtfully, then noticed the moisture on her brow. She reached up to touch it with surprise.

“You were praying quite intently,” Rurik observed, producing a handkerchief from his pocket. “May I?”

She nodded, sitting still as he gently wiped the perspiration from her face. “What requires such earnest supplication?” he asked softly.

“In time, we will see,” she replied simply.

“I believe you will.”

They sat in comfortable silence for a while, listening to the stream’s gentle murmur and the distant voices of his men around their fire. When Adelaide finished her meal, Rurik stood and extended his hand.

“You should rest. Tomorrow’s road grows more challenging.”

She accepted his assistance, then began to remove his coat. “Your warmth…”

“Keep it until you reach the carriage,” he insisted. “The night air has teeth.”

“Thank you.”
