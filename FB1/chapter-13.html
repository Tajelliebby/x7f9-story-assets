Adelaide woke to the sound of wheels crunching over something harder than dirt. She pushed aside the fur blanket Rurik had insisted she take and peered out the carriage window. The gentle rolling countryside had vanished during the night, replaced by something altogether more severe.

“We have reached the northern marches, My Lady,” Elaine said, noting Adelaide’s attention. Her icy blue eyes took in the transformed landscape with knowing recognition. “The very stones speak differently here.”

And they did. The scattered hamlets they passed bore walls of darker granite, carved deep with symbols Adelaide didn’t recognize. Runes, perhaps, their edges worn smooth by centuries of northern winds. Even the few travellers they encountered moved with a different purpose, bundled thick against cold that seemed to seep through everything, their faces bearing the particular hardness of those who survived by strength alone.

Adelaide pressed her palm against the frost-kissed glass. “How extraordinary. Even the air tastes different.”

“Crisper,” Elaine agreed, pulling her cloak tighter. Her sharp gaze lingered on Adelaide’s obvious fascination. “Are you quite comfortable, My Lady? The cold here seems to find every gap.”

“Perfectly fine.” Adelaide smiled, not moving from the window. She had slept better than expected, lulled by the memory of sitting beside a campfire with Rurik, speaking his name for the first time while he looked at her as though seeing someone entirely new. The warmth of that moment seemed to carry forward, even into this harsh landscape.

The road beneath them deteriorated with each mile. What had been proper flagstones gave way to packed earth, then to little more than muddy tracks studded with stones that made the carriage lurch and strain. Adelaide could hear their coachman muttering prayers under his breath as he fought to keep them moving forward.

“The roads feel rather lonesome,” she observed, watching the empty moorland stretch endlessly around them. “Shouldn’t there be more people?”

“That is the way of the north, My Lady,” Elaine replied. “Space between souls gives room to breathe, though I will grant it makes one feel rather small.”

As if summoned by her words, the landscape around them began its true transformation. The moorland gave way to something magnificent and terrible. Mountains rose like sleeping giants, their peaks shrouded in mist and crowned with snow that never melted. Dark heather clung to the lower slopes between stands of pine that grew sparser and more stunted as they climbed.

Adelaide caught her breath. These were the Giants’ Spines. She was certain of it. They pierced the very sky, their jagged silhouettes stark against the pale winter sun. “Are those truly where the old kings rest?” she asked Elaine.

“Indeed, My Lady. In cairns older than memory, they say. The Aurenfolk still make pilgrimages to them when the snows permit.”

The sound of the world had changed too. Gone were the familiar murmurs of civilized life: market chatter, cattle lowing, the comfortable rhythm of established order. Here, wind howled between peaks with a voice all its own. Birds of prey cried overhead, and somewhere in the distance water rushed down mountainsides with violent purpose. The silence between these sounds felt vast, as though the earth itself held its breath.

Snow began falling as they climbed higher, fat flakes that clung to the carriage windows and muffled the wheels. Adelaide watched it accumulate on the glass, remembering how delighted she’d been by her first glimpse of snow just days ago. Now it was becoming something more serious: drifts that made the already treacherous road nearly impassable.

“We may need to shelter soon,” Elaine observed, watching the snow fall with growing unease.

But the carriage lurched back into motion, wheels crunching through snow that was now ankle-deep. Adelaide felt that curious sensation in her left hand again, a tingling that had grown stronger with each mile north. She flexed her fingers, wondering at the strange energy building within her. Through the swirling snow, she caught glimpses of isolated crofts, their windows glowing like tiny beacons. The few inhabitants she saw moved with the spare purpose she was beginning to recognize in the northern people. Everything about them spoke of lives pared down to essentials, of beauty found in function rather than ornament.

“So this is Aurenfell,” she murmured, her breath fogging the glass. “So much harsher than anything we saw on the journey, yet…”

“Yet beautiful in its own way?” Elaine suggested with a slight smile.

“Yes, exactly.” Adelaide leaned closer to the window. “I begin to understand why His Grace speaks of it as he does.”

The mention of Rurik brought warmth to her cheeks that had nothing to do with the carriage’s brazier. Over their days of travel, she had come to see past his careful reserve to the man beneath, someone who carried the weight of responsibility with quiet dignity, who found purpose in protection rather than glory. When she had finally spoken his name by the campfire, she had seen something shift in his storm-grey eyes. A recognition, perhaps. An acknowledgment that she was not merely a guest to be escorted, but someone who might understand what it meant to serve something greater than oneself. The sun began its descent toward the western peaks, and the snow-laden forests took on an ethereal quality in the fading light. Pine boughs heavy with white created temple arches over glimpses of frozen streams, their surfaces locked in ice that gleamed like silver.

In the distance, Adelaide could make out the dark outline of what must be Frostspire Keep, its spires reaching toward the darkening sky like fingers of shadow. The sight sent an unexpected thrill through her, not fear, but anticipation. This would be her new home, the place where she would learn what it truly meant to serve as an Archon on the frontier. The tingling in her hand intensified, and she found herself studying the sensation with growing curiosity. It wasn’t unpleasant. It felt more like power awakening, like connection to something larger than herself.

“Elaine,” she said suddenly, her voice carrying an authority that surprised even her. “Down. At once.”

Elaine’s brows rose, though her tone remained steady. “My Lady?”

“Please.” Something in Adelaide’s expression must have convinced her, for the older woman dropped to the carriage floor without further question.

The carriage lurched violently, wheels skidding on ice. The horses screamed in panic, their breath misting heavily as they stamped and whinnied in terror. Beyond them, the forest stirred with ominous purpose, branches snapping like gunshots through the snow-laden pines.

Before Adelaide could see what had frightened the animals, Rurik’s voice cut through the chaos from outside. “Stay inside, My Lady! It is not safe!”

The urgency in his command made her heart race. Through the window, she could see all the soldiers with weapons drawn, their faces grim with recognition. She didn’t need to see the threat to understand what they faced. The very air thrummed with malevolent energy she had learned to recognize during her training.

Aedra.

A moment of absolute stillness settled over the scene. Then Adelaide saw the clearing ahead, snow that had been pristine white now soaked through with something dark and oily, its edges stained crimson where fallen knights lay motionless. The wind carried nothing but biting cold, as though even the forest held its breath in the presence of such corruption. Through the window, she watched Rurik’s jaw clench. Good men, his men, had fallen to these creatures. The weight of their sacrifice pressed visibly on his shoulders, and Adelaide felt an unexpected surge of protective anger on his behalf.

“Ready the men,” Rurik commanded his captain. “We push for Isgaldr Gate.”

Harald nodded grimly, already moving to rally the surviving knights. Blades rang from scabbards, and boots crunched through snow as soldiers took defensive positions. Adelaide watched Rurik spur his horse forward, scanning the shadows with predatory focus.

The shrill roar that tore through the stillness froze her blood.

A twisted figure emerged from the treeline with unnatural speed. All jagged limbs and bone-like protrusions, the creature leaped onto the lead knight, crushing him beneath its weight. Claws like black ice tore through armour as though it were parchment.

The knight died without even screaming.

“To arms!” Rurik roared, his sword already singing from its scabbard.

The forest erupted into chaos as more creatures surged from the shadows. They moved like nightmarish insects, far too quick for anything of this world. Adelaide remained pressed to the window, transfixed. She watched Rurik’s blade cut through corrupted flesh, dark blood spraying across pristine snow. Harald proved himself a force unto himself, his massive axe cleaving through demons with brutal efficiency. Yet even he couldn’t protect everyone, and Adelaide’s chest tightened as more knights fell.

“How did they penetrate so far?” she heard Rurik growl between sword strokes.

The energy in Adelaide’s hand built to painful intensity. The connection she’d been sensing grew stronger, more insistent, as though the land itself called to her. She pressed her palm against the carriage window, and the glass began to warm beneath her touch. Without conscious thought, she drew her travelling cloak around her shoulders and stepped down from the carriage. Her feet sank into snow, and cold bit at her exposed skin, but she barely noticed as she surveyed the battlefield.

“Gentlemen,” she called, her voice carrying clearly despite its gentle tone, “I suggest you retreat to the carriage if you do not wish to be consumed by judgment.”

Rurik’s head snapped toward her, his eyes wide. “Heed the lady’s words and retreat!” he commanded. As the knights pulled back, he noticed the remaining Aedra had gone completely still, shifting nervously. Then one broke away, scuttling rapidly toward the mountain pass. “No!” Rurik roared.

Adelaide raised her hand, and every man present covered his face as golden light blazed across the snow. Vines woven of pure radiance burst from the frozen ground, lashing about each creature and drawing tight. Where they closed, the Aedra burned with inhuman shrieks, consumed by holy light that left the surrounding trees untouched.

“Your Grace…” She pressed a hand to her nose and sneezed, then tightened her shawl. “Goodness, it is frightfully cold.”

Rurik dismounted quickly, wrapping his heavy coat around her shoulders. “Into the carriage with you,” he said firmly. “You’re freezing.” When she stumbled slightly, he gathered the coat tighter around her and lifted her easily, carrying her back to safety. Adelaide squeaked in surprise, then giggled, offering no resistance to his protective handling. “To horse!” he commanded after settling her inside. “We make for the gate!”

As the carriage resumed its journey, Adelaide burrowed into Rurik’s coat, surrounded by the scent of leather and pine and something indefinably masculine. She found herself glancing back the way they had come, sensing a lingering Aedran presence like a shadow across her consciousness. There would be answers to seek there, but not tonight.

The carriage climbed steadily higher, air growing thinner with each mile. Snow continued falling, creating a hushed world of white and shadow. The silence following battle felt profound rather than empty, as though the land acknowledged what had transpired. When the towering Isgaldr Gate finally came into view, night had settled over the peaks. The ceremonial entrance rose before them like something from legend, ancient stones carved with runes that pulsed faintly with their own inner light. The symbols spoke of traditions far older than the Empire, protective patterns that warded against both physical and magical threats.

“Magnificent,” Adelaide breathed, pressing close to the window once more.

As they passed beneath the carved lintel, she felt that curious tingling settle into a steady, warm presence. She was no longer simply travelling through Aurenfell. She had become part of it, connected to its ancient magic in ways she was only beginning to understand. The road ahead led to Frostspire Keep, to her new life as guardian of the northern frontier. Behind them, the Isgaldr Gate’s runes continued glowing softly in the darkness, standing sentinel against whatever shadows might dare follow.
