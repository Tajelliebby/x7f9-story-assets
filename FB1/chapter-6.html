They reached the palace courtyard, where Adelaide approached a coachman. Seeing where the exchange was headed, Rurik quickly intervened. “Surely we will not be using the same carriage,” his voice betraying more alarm than he intended.

“No?” She tilted her head, genuine confusion crossing her features.

He wanted to close his eyes in exasperation. Had she forgotten all court etiquette during her time on the front lines? The thought of them sharing a confined space, with all the wagging tongues the capital possessed…

“I’ll ride beside the coachman,” he found himself conceding yet again, wondering when he had lost his legendary resolve in the face of emerald eyes and golden curls.

“You don’t have to…?” she called as he moved to the front of the carriage, but he pretended not to hear her.

The coachman, a middle-aged man with weather-beaten skin, gave him a sidelong glance but said nothing as Rurik settled beside him. He nodded curtly, and the man flicked the reins, setting them in motion. From his elevated position, Valestra unfurled. Rurik gripped the edge of his seat as the carriage wheels rattled over cobblestones worn smooth by centuries of passage.

“First time seeing the capital proper, my lord?” the coachman asked, his hands deftly guiding the horses through the crowded streets.

“First time seeing it with eyes unclouded by war,” Rurik admitted, allowing his gaze to wander.

They passed beneath an archway where stone figures, ancient Guardians, gazed down upon them. Their pointed ears, like his own, marked them as blessed by the One Light, though the weight of such blessing often felt more curse than gift. Market-goers parted before their carriage, many bowing their heads in deference as they recognized the imperial seal. Traders from every corner of the empire had gathered, their stalls overflowing with exotic fruits and spices. Merchants called their wares from stalls adorned with vibrant fabrics that fluttered in the spring breeze. The air was thick with competing aromas: sweet pastries, roasting meats, pungent herbs and baking bread mingled with the earthy perfume of Adelaide’s flowers that seemed to follow her wherever she went.

“Good day!” he heard the lady chirp.

His gaze moved to a group of children crowded around a puppeteer performing the Tale of Creation, their wooden Iltheris figure raised high above a miniature world.

“The Archon Spring’s a regular sight in these parts,” the coachman commented, nodding toward a cluster of children who enthusiastically returned her greeting as they passed. “Doesn’t stand on ceremony, that one.”

Rurik hummed noncommittally. Adelaide’s voice drifted up from inside the carriage. “Oh, look at how they’ve decorated for the festival!”

Despite himself, he glanced down to see garlands of fresh flowers spanning the cobblestone streets, their vibrant colours a stark contrast to the grey stone buildings. Smoke curled from countless chimneys, creating a hazy canopy above the bustling thoroughfares. They turned onto a narrow street lined with elegant shopfronts, their windows displaying finery that would bankrupt lesser nobles. The coachman drew the horses to a halt before a boutique whose sign bore a golden needle threaded with silver.

“The Gilded Thread, my lord,” he announced. “Finest tailor in the Eastern Quarter. They dress the Archons for all ceremonies.”

Reluctantly, Rurik descended from his perch, but Adelaide had already leapt out of the carriage, adjusting her skirt. A bell chimed softly as Adelaide pushed open the boutique door. The interior was awash in silks and velvets of every hue, mannequins draped in half-finished creations that would soon grace the bodies of Valestra’s elite.

A young woman greeted them with a polite, somewhat lacklustre smile, her eyes widening slightly as she recognized his companion. “Your Eminence,” she curtsied. “How may we assist you today?”

Adelaide, to his surprise, seemed unbothered by the lukewarm greeting. “Is Madame Bertillon available? We have rather urgent business.”

Before the attendant could respond, a portly woman emerged from behind a curtain of midnight-blue fabric, her silver hair piled atop her head like a crown. “Is that Adelaide Veridis I hear? Come to torment me with another impossible deadline?”

Adelaide laughed and embraced the woman. “Madame Bertillon, forgive the imposition, but circumstances have changed rather suddenly.” She gestured toward him. “May I present Duke Rurik Iskaldsson of Aurenfell. We require complementary attire for the festival.”

The seamstress’s shrewd eyes assessed him from head to toe, missing nothing. “The Northern Guardian himself,” she murmured, offering a respectful curtsy. “A pleasure, Your Grace.”

He inclined his head. “Madame.”

“Since the Archons’ outfits were completed weeks ago, I was hoping you might have a suit for the Duke that could be altered to complement mine,” Adelaide hummed. “We haven’t time for a new creation from scratch.”

The seamstress laughed, her eyes crinkling at the corners. “Always considering my poor fingers, aren’t you, child? Most nobles would demand the impossible.”

“You know I cannot be inconsiderate to you, Madame,” Adelaide replied, her tone softening. “Not after all you’ve done for me and my siblings.”

“Come then, Your Grace,” Madame Bertillon beckoned. “Let us see what we can accomplish with our limited time.”

As they moved toward a measuring area, Rurik noticed the appraising glances of several younger seamstresses. Their eyes held an appreciation that made his skin crawl. The sensation was familiar and unwelcome.

“Madame,” he said quietly, “might I request that you personally attend to my measurements?”

Surprise flickered across her face, followed by understanding. “Of course, Your Grace. Ladies, attend to Archon Veridis’s refreshments.”

She led him to a private fitting room, its walls lined with mirrors that reflected his discomfort from every angle. As she stretched her measuring tape across his shoulders, Madame Bertillon spoke conversationally. “Northern blood shows in your build. Sturdy as the mountains, you lot.”

“Indeed, Madame.” He kept his gaze fixed on his own reflection. “You’ve known her long, then?”

“Since she was no higher than my knee,” Madame Bertillon chuckled, wrapping the tape around his chest. “Arms up, if you please, Your Grace.” He complied, and she continued, “She’s been coming to my shop alone or with her siblings since she was chosen. Never with a gentleman, though. Born and raised in Sundermarsh. Oh how I miss her tawny glow, the capital has made her so pale.”

Sundermarsh? Not the Capital?

“Is she finally growing up, I wonder?” the seamstress continued.

“I’m not entirely sure,” Rurik admitted.

The old woman’s hands stilled momentarily, her eyes meeting his in the mirror with sudden sharpness. “Not sure?” she repeated, her tone carefully neutral.

He realized too late the implication of his words. “Our… arrangement is quite recent,” he clarified, unwilling to elaborate further on matters he scarcely understood himself. “Fortune has woven its threads most cleverly,” he echoed the Emperor’s words, finding them apt.

“Is that so?” she asked, her tone measured. After a moment of scrutiny that made him feel as though she peered directly into his soul, she nodded once. “Well then, Your Grace. Perhaps it’s fortunate that you’ve come to me today.”

“How so, Madame?”

“Fabric reveals the man, Your Grace,” she said cryptically, returning to her measurements. “And the right suit might reveal parts of yourself even you have yet to discover.”

He swallowed, feeling strangely exposed despite being fully clothed. “I shall defer to your wisdom, Madame Bertillon.”

“Wise indeed,” she chuckled. “Now, tell me, how comfortable are you with the colour green?”

His traitor mind flashed unbidden to Adelaide’s sparkling eyes, spring leaves dappled with morning light. He closed his own against the unwelcome thought. “I am comfortable with it,” he managed, his voice steadier than he felt.

Madame Bertillon’s knowing chuckle sent heat to his collar. The woman possessed an uncanny ability to read a man’s thoughts, though he prayed to the One Light she hadn’t divined his completely.

When he emerged from the dressing room sometime later, an unexpected sight greeted him. Adelaide knelt on the polished floor beside one of the employees, the very one who had greeted them with such lacklustre enthusiasm. Her hand moved in gentle circles across the young woman’s back while the other squeezed her wrist. Two other attendants hovered nearby, one holding a basin with steady hands as the afflicted woman emptied the contents of her stomach. Adelaide whispered words of comfort, too soft for him to discern but carrying the rhythmic cadence of prayer.

“What’s wrong with Lisbette?” Madame Bertillon hurried toward them, her face etched with concern.

Adelaide turned toward them, and the serenity in her expression froze him. It was not the expression of the impish, boundary-testing woman he had begun to know. “The Light works in mysterious ways,” she replied, her eyes glowing faintly with the same ethereal radiance he had glimpsed during the banquet. “I sensed something amiss. It appears our Lisbette here has been severely ill for some time.”

She returned her attention to the young woman, who gazed up at her with tear-streaked adoration. “Thank you, Lady Archon. Thank you,” Lisbette whispered between shuddering breaths.

Adelaide shook her head. “Only the Light is to be praised.”

Rurik stood motionless, caught between the urge to approach and the feeling that he witnessed something too sacred for his intrusion. Once Lisbette was settled in a chair with a cup of mint-steeped water, they prepared to depart. The entire staff lined their path to the door, bowing deeply as they passed. To his surprise, Adelaide lowered her head to them as well.

As they stepped into the late afternoon air, Adelaide turned to him and released a sigh that seemed to deflate her entire being. Her shoulders drooped slightly as she looked to the sky. “Wonderful, is it not, that He intervened when He did?” she asked, her voice carrying a weariness absent moments before.

“Yes,” he nodded slowly, noting the shadows beneath her eyes that he hadn’t observed earlier. Whatever power she had channelled seemed to have exacted a toll. “Perhaps you should retire for today?”

“Yes, surely,” she agreed without protest, a first since their acquaintance began. He moved automatically toward the front of the carriage, prepared to resume his position beside the coachman when he felt a light tug at his sleeve. Adelaide had caught the fabric between her fingers. “Please accompany me this time.”

His lips parted to refuse, but concern for her condition overrode his discomfort. “I will concede,” he heard himself say, another in a growing collection of compromises that troubled his sense of self-possession.

Adelaide’s responding smile restored some colour to her pallid cheeks. “Thank you.”

And so they found themselves seated across from one another within the confines of the carriage, the space between them measured in mere feet yet feeling both too intimate and strangely vast. As the vehicle lurched into motion, Adelaide turned her attention to the window. The carriage wheels clattered over cobblestones, the rhythm punctuating the silence between them. He studied her profile, the gentle curve of her jaw, the delicate point of her ear visible when the breeze lifted her golden hair.

“The Northern Territories have nothing like this, I imagine,” she said without turning from the window.

“No,” he replied, his gaze following hers to the bustling street beyond. A woman walked past with a basket of violets. “Our settlements are more… austere. Necessity rather than celebration guides our construction.”

A small smile touched her lips. “Yet beauty finds its way even there, does it not?”

“Indeed,” he conceded, his voice softening with unbidden fondness. “My home is beautiful.”

Her gaze shifted to him then. “You must miss it,” she observed.

“Yes.” Then an earlier question resurfaced. “You gave me a knight’s salute earlier. In the Emperor’s study. Why?”

She looked at him then, and something flickered in her green eyes. Surprise, perhaps, that he’d noticed such a detail. “Because I am one,” she said simply.

“A knight?”

“Yes,” she confirmed, her chin lifting slightly. “Sworn to defend the realm and uphold the Light’s justice.” Another piece of the puzzle that was Adelaide Veridis. Suddenly she giggled, gaze returning to the roads. “I cannot wait to see it.”

“See what?”

“Aurenfell. Your home.” Her eyes met his again, bright with curiosity. “Will you show me the mountains? The forests you spoke of?”

Something tightened in his chest at the thought of Adelaide in his ancestral halls, her spring warmth bringing life to the stark beauty of his northern stronghold. “If you wish it,” he said quietly.

“I do.”
