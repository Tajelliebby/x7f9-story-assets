The days left to prepare for a festival were barely adequate, but they managed. The attack on the imperial palace had interrupted their preparations, though thankfully not destroyed them entirely. Adelaide woke feeling stiff from the previous day’s last-minute arrangements, her muscles protesting as she stretched.

“Let’s be quick, then,” she told her maids, offering a weak smile. “The sun awaits no maiden’s lingering.”

She washed hastily and was dressed in the traditional layers of white, each piece carefully arranged for the morning’s consecration prayers. As she walked with her fellow Archons toward the Summit of Seasons, her thoughts drifted to Rurik. Imagine, the youngest getting married first. The notion had never crossed her mind before, but she found herself rather pleased by it. He was quite handsome, after all.

They gathered at the Summit as dawn painted the sky in gentle hues. The others dispersed to their respective prayer chambers, leaving Adelaide alone before the great floor-to-ceiling window. Beyond the glass, the heavens whispered promises of the coming sun while the land below remained wrapped in darkness. A cool breeze touched her cheek, and she let her eyes drift closed. She whispered her uncertainty, should she go to Aurenfell or back to Risenholt, when suddenly she felt a sharp pain in her chest.

“O Blessed Light, keeper of the world, guardian of the faithful,” she whispered, her voice reverent yet trembling. “Let me love You as You have loved us. Let me serve…”

The prayer died in her throat as agony speared through her shoulder. Her forehead struck the cold marble with a sharp crack, and her muscles convulsed beyond her control. The familiar scent of sulphur choked her, mixing with the metallic tang of blood that flooded her mouth.

These are only memories, she told herself desperately. They cannot harm…

But she was falling. Not onto the sacred chamber’s floor, but into something else entirely. Snow. Endless white stretched to every horizon. The sky burned with fire and smoke, thick plumes twisting heavenward like writhing fingers. Crimson bloomed against the frozen ground in visceral contrast. Her hands clawed at the ice, but the cold brought no relief to the deep ache in her bones.

“I am not the seer,” she gasped, dry heaves wracking her body. “I am the healer. Why…”

“Adelaide!”

A firm hand gripped her shoulder, yanking her back to reality with jarring force. Her eyes flew open as she struggled to push herself upright, palms slick with sweat against the marble.

Dawn had broken. How long had she been lost?

“Sister!” Rowena’s voice carried urgency tempered with gentleness as she pulled Adelaide into her embrace. “Breathe. You’re safe.”

Safe? Adelaide thought bitterly. Are any of us truly safe? A broken sob escaped her throat as Rowena held her, rocking gently until the tremors subsided.

The Light had spoken, and the message was clear. She must go north to Aurenfell.

Time was slipping away, but Rowena insisted Adelaide compose herself before appearing before the citizens. A second outfit had been prepared for precisely such emergencies, pristine and untouched. Yet beneath the carefully arranged folds, her limbs still trembled. As they readied the open-backed carriage, Adelaide gazed out at the city. The trees, the flowers, everything seemed muted, dulled. First the attack on the capital, now this vision. She knelt briefly, hands clasped, but no words came. Only memories of blood-stained snow and distant screams.

When she looked up, the smoke of her vision still seemed to smoulder against the pale morning sky.

“Do you see it?” Adelaide asked.

Rowena did not look at her. Her eyes were closed, her hands clasped in prayer. “No. Perhaps I will.”

Slowly Adelaide lowered her eyes. “Sister Rowena…”

“Do not,” Rowena said, her eyes still closed. “It is not yet time. Do not utter it into the winds.” Adelaide shut her mouth at once, nodding even though Rowena could not see it. Only when Rowena finally opened her eyes did she rise, her expression unreadable as she rested a hand on Adelaide’s back. “Are you certain you do not wish to rest?”

“Do you rest after a vision?” Adelaide countered.

Rowena considered it. “Yes, if the vision is strenuous.”

Adelaide let the thought settle before shaking her head. “I have duties to fulfil.”

Understanding flickered in Rowena’s gaze, and she patted Adelaide’s head before stepping away. At seven o’clock precisely, the prayers began. Thorne, as eldest and Chief of the Archons, led the congregation. His deep, unwavering voice filled the square, weaving solemnity and reverence into the very air. As the final amen was spoken, the festival began in earnest. The Order presented their open-backed carriage, and the four Archons climbed aboard. Adelaide and Rowena lifted their hands, calling upon the Light, and divine petals cascaded from the heavens. Beside them, Kaelen and Thorne crafted light into living art that shimmered against the morning sky.

Joy began to replace Adelaide’s earlier dread. The citizens lined the streets, children with eyes bright as newly minted coins, adults whose faces bore the marks of hope. This was why they endured. Why she pushed past exhaustion and fear. A collective gasp rose from the crowd, followed by cheers of delight. Adelaide startled as the trees lining their procession burst into impossible bloom, barren branches suddenly heavy with blossoms. She caught her siblings’ eyes, and for a moment they simply gaped at one another before bursting into laughter.

Look at the promise of the Light, she thought. A sign that indeed He is still with us.

The procession wound through the capital’s grand streets until they arrived before the imperial palace. The towering gates stood open, revealing the royal family waiting atop the palace steps, bathed in golden morning light. The final act was the blessing of the imperial family. Each Archon would pronounce a different blessing upon the throne.

Thorne stepped forward first. “May wisdom increase.”

Kaelen followed. “May strength be ever renewed.”

Rowena lifted her hands. “May prosperity flow unceasing.”

Adelaide drew a deep breath. “May the Light’s protection never wane.” She placed a gentle hand on the emperor’s shoulder, whispering, “I know there is much to be fearful of, but do not doubt the Light.”

His eyes searched hers before a smile curved his lips. He nodded, accepting her words, taking them to heart. As the ceremony concluded and cheers swelled around them, Adelaide met Rurik’s eyes across the crowd. He offered a small nod that she found rather endearing before she followed her siblings away.

Across the square, a duke watched a small figure in white vanish among her siblings, and neither of them could have said who had looked away first.

Rurik watched Adelaide’s every step until she disappeared from sight. He had been surprised to see her in the flowing ceremonial dress and veil during the procession, though he supposed the Archons would change now and be able to enjoy the festivities properly. But goodness, did he wish he did not have to remain confined to this tent with men discussing money as soon as the priests were out of sight.

And here I thought the north was irreverent…

The conversation had been a blur of politics, alliances, and trade agreements that held little bearing on his northern domain. He had remained silent, content to listen and observe. It was far too early for this manner of society. Whatever political games these men played, he had little interest in joining. He glanced at the liquid in his cup. Just water, he realized, reflecting his grey suit with green accents. The April weather in the capital was not as cool as the north, so he appreciated Adelaide’s consideration with the common frock, a simpler and looser-fitting coat with cropped trousers.

Taking a sip of his lukewarm water, he tucked his free hand in his pocket. When the formalities paused, the older men, friends of his late father, turned their attention to him. “Yes?” he said, resigned.

“Absolutely marvellous swordwork at the ball, Your Grace!” Duke Lawson’s boisterous voice echoed through the tent, his enthusiasm as overwhelming as his cologne. He thrust his crystal goblet skyward, port wine sloshing dangerously close to the rim. “By heavens, it has been an age since I witnessed such masterful bladework! The way you drove back those otherworldly beasts, it was poetry in motion, I say!”

Rurik offered the barest inclination of his head, hoping his reserved response might temper the duke’s exuberance.

Viscount Eve chimed in smoothly. “Indeed, your presence proved most fortuitous, my lord Duke.” He paused to adjust his perfectly starched cravat. “Though I must observe that the Great Archons displayed remarkable prowess, particularly considering their recent return from the frontier. Their vigour was… most impressive.”

“You may be correct,” Rurik conceded slowly, his mind wandering to that night, to her.

“The Winter Archon especially!” Lawson leaned forward in his chair. “Barely raised his hand, and the Aedra fell like flies! Frozen solid, scattered about like common garden detritus!”

Rurik had not paid much attention to the ground-level skirmishes. Adelaide’s safety had consumed his thoughts, blinding him to all else.

Viscount Eve gracefully redirected their discourse. “The Spring Archon was equally remarkable,” he mused with careful consideration. “I confess, I was rather taken aback by her youth initially. And her… shall we say, unconventional manner of addressing the royal family.”

Lawson’s laugh held all the subtlety of breaking glass. “Unconventional! Ha! That’s one way to phrase it!”

Eve continued as though he had not heard. “Nevertheless, she has most assuredly earned her place among the Archons. The way she commanded the very trees themselves, truly extraordinary.”

“I was unaware you had made such careful study of the matter, Viscount,” Rurik commented, unable to keep the edge from his voice.

Eve’s eyebrow arched elegantly, though his chuckle held no malice. “How could one not? Such power commands attention, does it not?”

Rurik hummed agreement. Indeed, power like that did not go unnoticed. Their words became distant as his eyes fell to his drink, bringing him back to that moment he had noticed her dress, sheer under the ballroom’s flickering light. What had caught his attention was not the gown itself. It was her back. Her muscles had flexed, and a soft, otherworldly glow spread across her skin from the lines etched into her flesh. It must be the Mark of the Covenant he had read about.

“Power!” Lawson scoffed, drawing Rurik’s reluctant attention. “And beauty besides! Had you but seen how she carried herself, it is a terrible shame the Archons rarely wed. And even if she were to…”

“I advise caution in your next words, Duke,” Rurik said, his tone sharper than intended. “Her Eminence is no court flower to be admired. She deserves respect for what she’s achieved, not for how she looks while doing it.”

Lawson blinked like a startled owl, but Eve smiled knowingly. “Excellently put, Duke Aurenfell,” he murmured, raising his glass.

“Yes, yes, quite right,” Lawson hastily agreed. Then his face brightened with renewed enthusiasm. “Her power was indeed the true marvel! As we were being led to safety, I witnessed it myself. The branches! They enveloped the entire palace like a cocoon!” His hands flew through the air, sketching shapes that bore little resemblance to reality.

Does this man’s mouth not tire?

“It was like stepping into one of those legendary Bartusch kingdoms! Those elvish cities woven into the very trees themselves! Absolutely breathtaking! Though,” Lawson was in full flight, slapping his knee with enough force to make nearby cups rattle, “observe the palace today. Restored to its ordinary grandeur. I find myself rather missing the spectacle, if truth be told.”

“Nothing to grieve over. It would be a shame if roots lingered about the palace, wouldn’t it?” Rurik’s head snapped toward the voice. Adelaide approached with that familiar mischievous glint in her eyes. When she arrived, she curtsied properly. “It’s been a while, Duke Lawson Mirevale and Viscount Eve.”

“Bah! Just Lawson is fine,” the duke said with uncharacteristic humility.

Adelaide giggled, but Rurik did not take his eyes off her. When those green eyes lifted to meet his, her smile widened. “What?”

“Nothing,” he lied. Her eyes did not seem as bright as usual. He wanted to ask if she was well, but would not dare imply any weakness in front of others.

She snorted, actually snorted, which he had never seen a noblewoman do, and then laughed at his bewildered expression. Reaching up, she touched the green brooch pinned to his lapel, just over his breast pocket. “Oh, this one is quite nice.” She traced the silver chain with her fingertip.

“It went with the green and grey,” he explained rather dumbly.

Adelaide giggled again. “Yes, quite nicely. Come, I must show you around before the lantern lighting.” She took him by the wrist cuff and stepped forward. Rurik closed his eyes with a sigh. Adelaide jerked and nearly fell backward when he did not move with her. She looked at him confused, and Rurik vaguely motioned toward the duke and viscount, who watched with poorly concealed amusement. “Ah.” She curtsied again. “A good day to you both. Enjoy the festival.”

Lawson laughed heartily and returned her sentiment. Viscount Eve nodded behind his glass. Rurik nodded to them both, and then he and Adelaide moved away to explore the festival.
