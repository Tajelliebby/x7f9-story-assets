Adelaide

Rurik let himself take her in as she walked beside him. Her hair was pinned half up and flowed down her back; he was unsure if the vines twined in the knot were real or decorative, but they paired well with the green accents of her dress. She wore no jewellery, letting the simple elegance speak for itself. The dress was white with a high waistline positioned under the bust, creating a draped effect from the shoulders down. A lightweight green half cloak hung from her shoulders, worn by all the Archons with their festival attire. Common enough for an afternoon stroll, he supposed, but it looked infinitely more refined on her. Ironic, considering she was anything but conventional.

“I know we are to be betrothed, but it has not yet been announced,” he said mildly. “How much longer do you intend to hold onto me?” Adelaide looked at him, then let go with an apology he wasn’t sure she meant.

They spent the day visiting stalls and sampling street food. He was surprised her tiny frame could pack away so much until she explained she’d fasted that morning.

“I like to think I have a healthy appetite,” she said with a grin.

Rurik chose not to comment.

They interacted with children and visited merchants. “Tell me about this festival,” he said as they walked between stalls, Adelaide pausing to admire a merchant’s display of candied fruits. “What is the Radiant Festival to your people?”

Adelaide’s fingers traced the edge of a sugar-dusted pear, but her attention turned fully to him. “It celebrates our connection to our Creator,” she said, her voice taking on a reverent quality. “The prayers, the procession, the lanterns… they remind everyone that the Light hasn’t forsaken us, especially now with the Aedra’s presence growing stronger.”

Rurik watched her face as she spoke. “And you believe this? That a single divine force governs all of nature?”

“I do.” She snapped the pear, offering him half. “The elements we command aren’t magic, Your Grace. They’re manifestations of divine will. Vines do not move because I ask it; they move because I speak the Light’s will. Were it not His will, they would not move.”

He accepted the sweet, studying her. In the north, they had their gods, old ones, tied to winter and war, but nothing quite like this absolute devotion. “You speak as though you know this Light personally.”

Adelaide’s cheeks coloured slightly, and she looked away. “In a way, I suppose I do. He… He treats me as a father would his child.” Her voice dropped to almost a whisper. “He even scolds me when I act improperly.” The admission was so unexpected, so endearingly human, that Rurik found himself chuckling before he could stop himself. Adelaide’s head snapped up, her eyes wide. “Why do you laugh?”

He met her gaze directly, honesty compelling him forward. “Because, my lady, you strike me as the sort of child who would require frequent scolding.”

For a moment, silence stretched between them. Then Adelaide burst into giggles, covering her mouth with her hand. “That may be true,” she admitted, her eyes dancing with mirth. “I fear I am rather inclined toward… impulsive decisions.”

“Rather,” Rurik agreed dryly, thinking of how she’d volunteered herself as his bride.

As the sun started to descend, she startled. “Oh! We may be late because of me.” She grabbed his cuff again and clutched her skirt, beginning to run.

Rurik was sure something nearly ruptured watching her dash through the streets in her fine dress, but he wasn’t about to correct her now, seeing as they were apparently late for something important. Soon they reached a river where many had gathered. After looking about, Adelaide spotted the other Archons.

“I thought you wouldn’t make it,” Kaelen joked.

“I wouldn’t miss it,” she replied breathlessly. They collected a lantern, and Adelaide explained they needed to write a prayer on it first. Rurik wasn’t quite sure what to put while Adelaide looked as though she didn’t have space for all her requests. “If you don’t have anything to ask of the Light,” she said, noticing his hesitation, “then write a few words of thanks.”

Rurik paused, then nodded. He liked the idea. He wrote a few lines of gratitude while Adelaide scribbled what appeared to be a lengthy correspondence. Adelaide held the lantern steady while Rurik lit it. Then Rowena stepped forward for the blessing, her voice carrying over the gathered crowd. On her “amen,” all the lanterns were set gently on the water’s surface.

Rurik found himself in awe of the beauty: hundreds of lanterns glowing warmly as they floated down the river like fallen stars. Then Rowena opened her hands and spoke words he didn’t understand. The winds picked up dramatically, and Rurik watched in wonder as some of the lanterns were swept up into the night sky. There were cheers from some onlookers, tense expressions from others.

“What does it mean?” he asked quietly.

Adelaide’s eyes followed the ascending lights. “The lanterns that are lifted are the prayers favoured by the Light.”

Understanding dawned. Rurik looked quickly to see if their lantern still floated on the river. It didn’t. Relief flooded through him as he realized his prayer of gratitude, thanking the Light for sparing Adelaide that night, had been among those chosen.

“What did you pray for?” Adelaide asked softly, watching their lantern climb toward the stars.

Rurik looked at her profile, illuminated by the warm glow of a hundred floating lights. “That the Light would continue to protect what matters most.”

She turned to him, golden lights reflecting in her curious green eyes. “And what matters most, Your Grace?”

He met her gaze steadily. “I believe you know the answer to that, Lady Adelaide.”

“Do I?” She held her chin, nose wrinkled in deep thought, and Rurik chuckled.

Above them, their lantern disappeared into the night sky, carrying their prayers toward whatever divine ear might be listening. Around them, the festival continued in gentle celebration, but for this moment, they existed in their own small world of possibility and hope.

“Come,” Adelaide said eventually, tugging his sleeve. “There’s still dancing by the palace gardens. And I have it on good authority that you’re not entirely hopeless at it.”

“Good authority?” Rurik raised an eyebrow.

“I may have asked the Emperor,” she admitted with characteristic mischief. “For the sake of preparation, naturally.”

“Naturally,” he agreed dryly. Who asks the Emperor if a Duke can dance? “After you, my lady.”

Rurik spent the remainder of the festival with Adelaide, watching her sample honeyed cakes from vendors and laugh with children who’d escaped their nursemaids. The evening wore on, and he noticed her eyes beginning to droop despite her efforts to stifle yawns behind her hand. For a moment he dared think she looked rather endearing with the way she pouted when tiredness overtook her enthusiasm, but he quickly brushed the thought aside. He caught the attention of a passing servant and requested they locate one of the other Archons.

Leading Adelaide to a stone bench beneath a flowering tree, he sat carefully. Immediately, she rested her head against his forearm, apparently giving little thought to propriety. He wasn’t even surprised any longer. She seemed to regard social conventions as mere suggestions. He sat as still as possible, watching the festivities continue around them. Nobles danced and drank wine from crystal goblets, speaking cheerily to one another. Common blessings floated through the evening air. Everyone appeared to be in uncommonly good spirits.

“Thank you for staying with her.” Rurik looked up to find Thorne approaching with a gentle smile, a woolen blanket draped over his arm.

“It was my honour,” Rurik replied quickly.

Thorne waved the formality away with a chuckle. “You needn’t do that with me.” He moved to drape the blanket carefully over Adelaide’s sleeping form. “I may be a born noble, but that matters little once you join the Order.”

Rurik nodded. He understood how it worked, you lost your family name upon taking vows. If you became an Archon, the only surname you could claim was that of your assigned season. Nobles lost names, commoners gained them.

The thought stirred something in him. He moved his lips hesitantly before blurting, “I have a question.”

Thorne paused in tucking the blanket around Adelaide’s shoulders. “Go ahead.”

“Is it possible for us to… that is, can she marry?” Thorne blinked. “She’s an Archon,” Rurik expressed his doubt.

“Nothing prevents it,” Thorne smiled slightly. “Are you perhaps thinking of vows of chastity?”

Heat crept up Rurik’s neck. He simply nodded.

Thorne chuckled softly. “In that case, you have nothing to fear. Adelaide never took such vows. They’re not mandated by the Order.”

Rurik… had not known that. Understandably, he was learning quite a bit now that he found himself involved rather than merely observing from the sidelines. “I see.”

“Any other questions?”

“None that I can think of presently.”

Thorne offered a comforting smile. “In that case, good evening to you. Go safely home.”

“Yes, thank you.” Rurik watched as Thorne gently roused Adelaide and led her away, her steps still drowsy but steady. The moment the Archons departed, Rurik found himself with no desire to remain among the celebrating nobles. He headed directly for his chambers.

“Your Grace.”

Rurik paused at seeing the giant of a man stationed beside his door. Harald stood nearly seven feet tall, his massive frame draped in the practical leather and wool of the northern territories. Shaggy brown hair fell past his shoulders, and his beard was tied in three precise knots, a tradition from his village of Bearhook. Scars crisscrossed his weathered hands and what was visible of his neck above his collar. His cold grey eyes never betrayed his thoughts, earning him the nickname “The Stone Wall” among Rurik’s household guard.

“Shall we prepare to depart?” Harald asked.

The duke studied his captain and longtime friend. Despite Harald’s imposing presence, Rurik had always found comfort in the man’s silent, unyielding loyalty.

“No,” Rurik finally answered. “I think I’ll remain for a day or two.”

Harald’s eyes widened fractionally before he composed himself. “Understood.”

“Send the swiftest of our knights as messenger.” Harald nodded, though his eyes asked for explanation. “Joachim and Henrik need to know to make arrangements.” Rurik paused at his door. “To welcome my betrothed to Aurenfell.”

Rurik didn’t notice Harald’s eyes widen considerably as he retreated to his chambers for the night, his mind already turning to preparations for the coldest, most unforgiving territory in the empire. After sealing his letter with wax, he dismissed the offered assistance and prepared his own bath. No need for maids. He preferred they stay clear of his person entirely.

The sunken marble tub filled, and he added the pine oils he had carried from home, releasing a sharp, familiar scent. He descended the shallow steps and sank into the steaming water, a sigh escaping him that seemed to rise from the very depths of his being. His eyes drifted closed, letting the tension bleed away into the water. But then, like smoke curling under a door, a different scent invaded his sanctuary: light, floral, utterly foreign to anything in his northern halls. Not the suffocating perfumes that clung to capital noblewomen like desperate promises, but something fresher. Softer. Clean as morning air over wildflowers.

Her image surfaced unbidden in his mind. That maddening smile, the way she’d spoken to him so freely. The more he tried to banish her from his thoughts, the more persistently she lingered.
