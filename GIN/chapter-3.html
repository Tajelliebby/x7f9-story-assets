The April morning carried that crisp bite that made Warsaw feel alive—all possibility and fresh starts. I stood outside Kaje's door at precisely seven-fifteen, Sebastian's carrier in one hand, my work bag slung over my shoulder, trying to ignore the way my stomach was doing gymnastics.

"Are you absolutely sure you don't mind?" I asked for what had to be the fifth time this morning when he opened the door. "Because I can figure something else out—"

"Juli." His voice held that patient warmth that made my chest do weird fluttery things. "I offered, remember? And if Sebastian turns out to be a complete menace, we'll work something out after today. But right now?" He tilted his head, studying my face. "You need to stop spiraling and focus on crushing your first day."

I peeked into the carrier where my traitorous cat was lounging like he owned the place. "Easy for you to say. You're not the one abandoning your child with a near-stranger."

"Ouch. Near-stranger?" Kaje clutched his chest in mock hurt. "After everything we've been through? The bookshelf assembly? Stumbling because of cardboard boxes?"

"You know what I mean," I mumbled, suddenly fascinated by Sebastian's judgmental expression.

"Do I?" His voice dropped, and when I looked up, something flickered in his eyes that made my pulse stutter. "Hey." His voice gentled, hand reaching out like he wanted to touch my arm but stopping just short. "Take the stairs in flats, switch to heels at the office or on the metro. You've got time if you leave now."

Always so damn practical. I kicked off my heels, shoving them into my bag. "Good call. Thanks."

"Go show them what Juliana Kowalczyk is made of."

"Bold of you to assume I know what I'm made of," I muttered, but his dimpled grin made warmth spread through my chest.

"I do. Go get 'em, tiger." I was halfway down the first flight when I heard him call out, "Sebastian and I will probably be best friends by the time you get back!"

The traitor mewed in agreement.

The metro from Stacja Lipowa was packed with the usual morning crowd—students with oversized headphones, office workers clutching takeaway coffees, tourists consulting their phones with that particular brand of determined confusion. I managed to snag a seat and switched back to my heels, trying to channel the confidence of the put-together woman I was pretending to be. Twenty-eight minutes later, I emerged at Stacja Centrum into the gleaming heart of Śródmieście Biznesowe. The business district stretched out in all directions—glass towers catching the morning light, well-dressed professionals power-walking with purpose. The air smelled like expensive coffee and ambition.

Białek Tower loomed above everything else, its minimalist silver "B" catching the light in a way that screamed money. My stomach clenched as I pushed through the revolving doors into a lobby floor that probably cost more than my university tuition.

"Juliana Kowalczyk," I told the receptionist, who looked like she'd stepped out of a fashion magazine. "First day, Financial Analysis."

She handed me a temporary badge with a smile that was polite but not particularly warm. "Eighth floor. Orientation room is to the right when you exit the elevators."

The elevator whisked me up with stomach-lurching efficiency. When the doors opened, I followed the signs to a glass-walled conference room where about ten other new hires sat around a polished table that could have doubled as a mirror. I slid into an empty chair, nodding at the woman next to me—blonde, nervous energy, kept checking her phone. Before I could introduce myself, the door opened and in walked a woman who radiated the kind of authority that made you sit up straighter without thinking about it.

"Welcome to Białek & Co.," she announced, her voice carrying easily through the room. "I'm Agnieszka Kowal, HR Director. For the next few hours, we'll cover everything you need to know about being part of the Białek family."

Family. Right. Because families definitely made you sign NDAs.

I was silencing my phone when I felt eyes on me. A man across the table was watching with an intensity that made my skin crawl. Dark hair, expensive suit, eyes that lingered on my mouth when I offered a polite smile. When I glanced up again after taking notes, he was still staring.

Great.

Orientation was a blur of corporate speak, benefits packages that sounded too good to be true, and safety presentations that made me wonder what exactly happened in this building. By the time Agnieszka dismissed us for lunch, my brain felt like it had been stuffed with cotton, and I was desperate for caffeine and fresh air.

As we filed out, Creepy Starer materialized at my elbow. "Juliana Kowalczyk, right?" His voice was smooth in that practiced way that made me think of car salesmen. "I'm Tomasz Zieliński. Management Consulting."

I shook his offered hand briefly. "Financial Analysis."

"Ah, a numbers girl." His grip lingered just long enough to cross the line from professional to weird. "We should grab coffee sometime. I could show you around, help you get settled—"

"Juli!"

Relief flooded through me as Zofia appeared like an angel in designer heels, her signature red lipstick perfect despite the morning chaos. "There you are," she said, linking her arm through mine with casual possessiveness. "I'm stealing you for lunch."

She offered Tomasz a smile that could have cut glass. "Zieliński."

"Nowak," he replied, his own smile tightening around the edges. "I didn't realize you two knew each other."

"Since diapers," Zofia said sweetly. "Juli, Sylwia's waiting downstairs. We made reservations."

"Of course." His eyes tracked my retreat with an intensity that made my skin crawl. "We'll catch up later, Juli."

I let Zofia lead me toward the elevators, offering Tomasz a brief wave. Once we were safely inside and the doors had closed, I exhaled shakily. "Is he always so..."

"Predatory? Like he's running calculations on the best way to get you alone?" Zofia jabbed the lobby button with more force than necessary. "Pretty much. Consider yourself warned."

Café Mokra was exactly the kind of place that made you feel both sophisticated and slightly intimidated—velvet chairs that probably cost more than my monthly grocery budget, baristas who looked like they'd judge your coffee order, and the kind of ambient lighting that made everyone look mysterious and important.

Sylwia waved from a corner table, her sleek bun making my hastily-pinned updo look amateur. "How's the first day treating you?"

"Well, I've already attracted the attention of the office creep," I sipped my overpriced cloud macchiato. "So that's... something."

"Zieliński?" Sylwia wrinkled her nose. "Ugh, he's the worst. Last year at the Christmas party, he cornered three different women by the coat check. HR finally had to step in."

"Charming. And here I thought my biggest worry would be remembering which elevator bank went to which floor."

"Oh, that's still a valid concern," Zofia said cheerfully. "I got lost for twenty minutes on my second day and ended up in the executive dining room. Nearly walked in on the CEO having lunch with some government minister."

We chatted about office politics and survival strategies, and for the first time all day, I felt like I could actually breathe. This was good. Normal. The kind of lunch break I'd imagined when I pictured my new life.

Then I noticed him.

An older man, maybe sixties, sitting alone at a table near the window. He wasn't being obvious about it, but he kept glancing in our direction. Not at Zofia or Sylwia—at me. When our eyes met, I offered a polite wave, more curious than concerned.

He smiled slightly and nodded back, but didn't look away.

"Okay, that's weird," I muttered.

"What?" Sylwia followed my gaze.

"Window guy. He's been watching me for ten minutes."

Zofia turned to look, then shrugged. "Probably just thinks you're pretty. You do clean up nice, Kowalczyk."

Maybe. But something about his attention felt different from Tomasz's predatory interest. I pushed the thought away. First-day paranoia, nothing more.

But that afternoon? Corporate hell.

Marek, my supervisor, spoke only in financial jargon and seemed allergic to human emotion. He dropped a stack of files on my desk thick enough to stop bullets. "Komorowski portfolio. Preliminary analysis by Wednesday. Wiesława will show you the systems." He nodded toward a stern woman who barely acknowledged my existence. "Questions?"

About a thousand, but something in his expression suggested now wasn't the time. "I'll figure it out."

"Good. Might need you after hours later this week if the situation changes. Keep your phone on."

By five, my brain felt blended. Numbers swam before my eyes, and I'd consumed enough coffee to power a small city. When Marek dismissed me with a curt nod, I practically ran for the elevator. The metro ride home was a blur of exhaustion and overstimulation. All I wanted was to collapse on my couch with Sebastian and pretend the adult world didn't exist for a few hours.

But first, I needed to collect my cat.

I knocked on Kaje's door, trying to summon what was left of my people energy. The door swung open, and my brain promptly short-circuited. He stood there, hair damp and tousled from the shower, water droplets still clinging to his neck and that chest. His t-shirt was somewhere between threadbare and transparent, clinging to muscles I definitely shouldn't be noticing.

"Hey," he said, that dimpled smile making everything worse and better at the same time. "How was the first day?"

I spun around so fast I nearly gave myself whiplash. "Good! Fine! I'll just—give you a second to—"

"Juli, chill." He laughed, "I'll bring Sebastian over in a bit."

I kept my eyes firmly fixed on the hallway wall until I heard his door close. Deep breaths. Professional thoughts. Tax codes. Quarterly reports. Anything but the image of— Nope. Not going there. A few minutes later, he emerged fully clothed, thank God, with Sebastian's carrier and a paper bag that smelled incredible.

"What's that?" I asked, leading him into my apartment.

"Dinner. Figured you might be too tired to cook." He released Sebastian, who immediately began his usual inspection tour of the apartment. "Also realized you don't actually have anything in your cupboards except cat food."

"Kaje, you didn't have to—"

"I know I didn't have to." He smiled. "I wanted to. Besides, we need to get you properly stocked up. Can't have you surviving on takeaway and desperation."

"Right..." I said weakly. "Grocery shopping."

"Grocery shopping," he agreed. "But not right now. You look like you're about to fall over. Go shower, decompress. We'll handle the adult responsibilities later."

The hot water helped wash away the first-day jitters and corporate anxiety. I pulled on my softest jeans and an oversized sweater, tied my hair up in a messy bun, and actually felt human again.

My balcony was small but cozy, with just enough space for my herb garden and a single chair. The evening air carried the scent of blooming trees and that particular spring energy that made everything feel possible. I watered my basil and mint, checking the new growth with satisfaction. The distance to Kaje's balcony was maybe around a metre.

Could I make it? Probably. Would it be incredibly stupid? Definitely.

I settled for admiring his jungle of plants from afar and went back inside. Sebastian had claimed his favorite spot on the windowsill, apparently unbothered by his day with a stranger. Traitor. A soft knock interrupted my mental preparation for grocery shopping. Kaje stood in the hallway with two reusable shopping bags, looking annoyingly put-together for someone who'd been shirtless an hour ago.

"Ready for the grand adventure of adult grocery shopping?" he asked.

I grabbed my keys and wallet. "Wait—what happened to all my cardboard boxes? The mountain of them?"

"Oh, those." He grinned. "I was going to take them to the recycling setup behind the building, but I ended up giving them to the giving nook instead."

"The what now?"

"Giving nook. I'll show you another time." He held the door open for me. "Come on, let's get you properly fed."

As we walked toward the store, chatting about everything and nothing, that familiar flutter started in my chest. The same one I'd had at fifteen whenever Cecylia's gorgeous older brother actually acknowledged my existence. The one I thought I'd buried under years of distance and supposed maturity.

Some crushes, apparently, just go into hibernation.
