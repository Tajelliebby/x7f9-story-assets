Juli

The two-minute walk to the grocery store felt like decompression after corporate hell. April air carried promises, and I could finally breathe properly. “So how was Sebastian as a houseguest?” I asked, falling into step beside Kaje.

His face lit up. “Total angel. Spent the day judging my plant arrangements and claiming the sunny spot. I think he’s decided I’m acceptable staff.”

“High praise from His Royal Fluffiness. He once held a three-day grudge for switching his food brand.”

“Well, if you’re cool with it, I can watch him tomorrow too. We’ve got a good thing going.”

The casual offer made something warm unfurl in my chest. “You sure? I don’t want to impose—”

“Juli.” I rubbed my neck at the teasing drawl. “I offered, remember? Besides, Sebastian and I are becoming fast friends. He’s excellent company.”

“Well, it’s your funeral.” Inside the store, we wandered aisles chatting about everything and nothing, and I kept getting distracted by how easy this felt. “God, I haven’t had proper żurek in forever,” I said, pausing by the soup section.

“My babcia makes the best. Secret ingredient.” He tapped his nose mysteriously.

“What is it?”

“If I told you, it wouldn’t be secret.”

“Tease.”

“You love it.”

And my stomach flipped. Focus, Juli. Groceries. Adult responsibilities.

But then he reached for something on the top shelf, honey, golden and perfect in the afternoon light streaming through the windows, and I caught sight of the lean muscle of his forearm. The way his shirt pulled across his shoulders, and my brain promptly short-circuited.

“You good?” He was looking at me with amusement. “You’ve been staring at that pasta for like, two minutes.”

“Just… deciding between shapes,” I lied, grabbing the first box within reach.

“Penne. Classic choice.”

By checkout, we’d discovered mutual obsessions with obscure Polish cinema, reality TV, and pierogi debates. Dangerous territory, this much common ground.

As the cashier started scanning my items, I remembered my mission. “Hold on— forgot something.”

I darted back toward the snack aisle, grabbing spicy kabanosy, chocolate-covered krówki, and a six-pack of Tyskie. Cat-sitting appreciation gifts. When I returned, Kaje was already settling the bill.

“Hey, no— let me pay for my stuff.”

“Already done.” He pocketed his receipt with a shrug.

“Kaje, seriously, let me pay you back—”

“Sure thing.” His tone suggested zero intention of accepting money.

Fine. I turned to the cashier with my haul. “These are separate.”

Kaje’s eyebrows shot up. “What’s all this?”

“Payment for putting up with Sebastian. And me.” I held up the beer. “Consider it appreciation.”

“For me?” Hand to chest, mock surprise. “Juli, you shouldn’t have.”

“Fine, then they’re mine,” I teased, reaching for the bag.

He tilted his head. “Ain’t that a shame.” A shiver ran up my spine. When had his voice gotten so that?

“On second thought, they’re definitely yours.”

Pure trouble, that grin.

Back home, Kaje led me behind the building to a cozy alcove I’d never noticed. “The giving nook,” he announced, gesturing to shelves organized by category and a sign reading “Take What You Need, Leave What You Can.”

“This is incredible.” I stared at books, household items, flattened cardboard. “It wasn’t on the website.”

“Pretty new. Resident initiative.” He pointed to my former box mountain. “They were running low.” In the corner, a small candle shaped like a sleeping cat caught my eye. White wax, painted details. “Take it,” Kaje said immediately. “That’s the whole point.”

Three flights later, easy when Kaje carried everything except my snacks, I was alone putting away actual adult groceries. The dinner he’d cooked, magical pasta with herbs I couldn’t identify, was better than anything I’d managed in my entire adult life. Restaurant-quality. With my stomach full and Sebastian purring on his favorite windowsill, I found myself pulling out my phone and downloading a dating app.

“This is smart,” I told Sebastian, who fixed me with a judgmental stare. “I need to meet people. Expand my social circle. Date someone who isn’t…” Sebastian’s tail twitched disapprovingly. “Don’t give me that look. It’s called being proactive.”

But as I scrolled through profiles, guys with gym selfies and quotes about loving adventures. I found myself comparing every smile to dimples I shouldn’t be thinking about, every bio to conversations about poetry and secret ingredients.

Get it together, Kowalczyk.

After cleaning up dinner, I grabbed Kaje’s empty tupperware and my thank-you gifts, padding across the hall in my fuzzy socks. His door opened before I’d finished knocking.

“Juli?” He looked surprised, “everything okay?”

I held up the beer. “Thought we could chill. If you’re not busy.”

“Never too busy for beer and good company.”

If I’d thought his apartment looked impressive from the doorway, being inside was like stepping into some kind of urban Eden. Every surface hosted plant life. Trailing pothos, herbs that made the air smell like heaven, a wall of greenery that belonged in a magazine. The rust-colored sofa looked impossibly inviting, surrounded by mismatched floor cushions and coffee table books about landscape design.

“Holy shit, Kaje. You actually terraformed your apartment.”

He laughed, that low rumble. “Bit much?”

“It’s incredible. How do you keep them alive? I’ve killed cacti.”

“Cacti are trickier than people think. Overwatering.” He opened two beers, handing me one. “Most plants just want consistency. Regular attention.”

“Ah. That explains my track record with… everything.” We settled on his sofa, me curled into one corner, him sprawled on the other, and he queued up some mindless comedy. The beer was cold, the couch was comfortable, and for the first time in weeks, I felt my shoulders actually relax. “Thanks for this,” I said during a lull in the movie. “Today was… a lot.”

“First weeks usually are.” His voice was gentle. “Getting easier?”

“Slowly.” I took a sip of beer. “My supervisor is intense, and there’s this guy…” I made a face. “Anyway, I’ll figure it out.”

“Want to talk about it?”

“Not really. I’d rather just… exist for a while. No thinking required.”

“I can do that.”

And he could. We sat in comfortable silence, the movie playing background to the sound of his plants rustling in the evening breeze through open windows. The beer made me loose-limbed and drowsy, and somewhere between the second act and credits, I felt my eyes getting heavy. The last thing I remembered was the warm weight of his hand briefly touching my hair.

My alarm blasted me awake in my own bed. How had I…?

Then it came back. Falling asleep on Kaje’s couch. Waking to his fingers gently stroking my hair, his voice soft: “Come on, sleepyhead. Let’s get you to bed.”

Mortification hit like a truck.

Shit, I groaned into my pillow.

Sebastian meowed from the doorway, and I realized I’d wasted precious time wallowing in embarrassment. I threw on yesterday’s blazer with fresh everything else, grabbed Sebastian, and knocked on Kaje’s door.

“Morning,” he said with that easy smile. “Sleep okay?”

“Great. Fine. Thanks for… you know.” I shoved Sebastian’s carrier at him. “I really need to go.”

“Juli—”

“See you tonight!” I called, already speed-walking toward the stairs.

Day two at Białek was worse than day one. Marek appeared before I’d removed my coat, dropping three files like personal insults. “The Komorowski analysis needs more detail. And I need you to look at these. Wiesława is out sick.”

By lunch, my brain felt like overcooked pierogi. I’d missed Zofia, trapped in pointless conference calls, and by the time I escaped to Café Mokra, it was nearly two. The place was mostly empty, just remote workers and that older man from yesterday. When I walked in, he looked up and smiled. I ordered caffeine strong enough to resurrect the dead and settled with my sad desk salad. Halfway through forcing down lettuce, a shadow fell across my table.

“Excuse me, is this seat taken?” The café was practically empty, but I nodded politely. He settled with grateful smile. “Thank you. Enjoying Warsaw? You have that look of someone still discovering the city.”

“Still settling in. Just started a new job.”

“Ah, the corporate armor.” His eyes twinkled, gesturing to my blazer. “What field?”

“Finance. You?”

“Retired. Spent my career in… consulting.”

Something about how he said it felt deliberately vague. We chatted, harmless small talk about neighborhood, weather, city life. But when I excused myself, I couldn’t shake the feeling he’d been studying me. Like trying to figure something out.

Back at the office, Tomasz materialized the moment I stepped off the elevator. “Juli! How was lunch? I know this great place—”

“Swamped with work, thanks.” I ducked around him, but he followed.

“Come on, all work and no play… Drinks after? I could show you better spots—”

“Really not interested.” I sat down, opened a file, hoping he’d take the hint.

He didn’t. For three hours, he hovered. Offering coffee, asking about progress, finding excuses to lean over my desk too close. When five hit, I was out faster than Sebastian hearing treats. Our building felt like safety. I climbed stairs two at a time, knocked on Kaje’s door, and when it opened, nearly collapsed with relief.

“Rough day?” he asked, taking in my frazzled expression.

“Understatement of the century.” I accepted Sebastian, buried my face in soft fur. “Can I complain at you? I’m at DefCon 1 stress levels.”

We settled on his couch and I launched into the miserable recap. Marek’s impossible expectations, Tomasz’s persistent creepiness, the weird interaction with Stanisław.

“The old guy could just be lonely,” Kaje said thoughtfully. “Retirement’s isolating. As long as he’s not crossing boundaries…”

“Yeah, figured that. But Tomasz gives me serious predator vibes.”

“Want me to have a word?”

My toes curled and I hugged Sebastian tighter. “What are you gonna do, intimidate him with your landscape architecture degree?”

“Hey, I can be intimidating. I’m very tall.”

“You’re a golden retriever in human form. Most threatening thing about you is your plant collection.” I was laughing when my phone rang. Expecting Marek, I answered without checking. “Juliana Kowalczyk speaking.”

“Juli, baby, please don’t hang up—”

I ended the call, nearly throwing the phone. Hands shaking, couldn’t get enough air. Sebastian appeared, pressing against my chest with concerned meows. Eyes closed, forcing breath. In for four, hold for seven, out for eight.

When I opened them, Kaje was crouched in front of me, expression gentle. “Ex?” he asked quietly. I nodded. “Want to talk about it?”

“God, no. I’d rather discuss root canals or economic collapse.”

He studied my face, then nodded. “Chicken and beer?”

Twenty minutes later, we sat on his living room floor with takeaway and cold Tyskie, and I somehow spilled everything. “He made me feel crazy,” I said, picking at chicken. “Like I was being dramatic. Sometimes I still wonder if he was right.”

“He wasn’t.” Kaje’s voice was firm. “Trust your gut, Juli. It’s trying to protect you.”

“I know that. Logically. But knowing and feeling are different things.” We ate in comfortable silence, knots in my shoulders slowly unwinding. Something about Kaje’s presence made everything manageable. “Sorry for being such a mess,” I said eventually, curling against his couch arm.

“Everyone needs an outlet. That’s what friends are for.”

Friends. Right.

“You’re such a good guy,” I mumbled, drowsy. “So happy you’re my neighbor.”

I wasn’t too far gone to hear, “Sweet dreams, Juli.”
